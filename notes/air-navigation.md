## 1. The Solar System

### The earth's orbit
- The earth is a planet moving in an **elliptical orbit** around the sun (sun at one **focus**). Each planet's year differs; the earth's year = **365 days 5 h 48 min 45 s**.
- **All heavenly bodies rise in the East** (the earth rotates west → east; seen from above the North Pole, **anticlockwise**).
- **Kepler's laws**: (i) planets move in ellipses with the sun at a focus; (ii) the line joining planet and sun sweeps **equal areas in equal times** → orbital speed is **fastest when closest** (perihelion), slowest when farthest (aphelion).
- **Perihelion** (closest) ≈ **beginning of January** (NH winter, SH summer). **Aphelion** (farthest) ≈ **beginning of July** (NH summer, SH winter).

### Tilt and seasons
- The spin axis is tilted **66.5° to the plane of the orbit** (= 23.5° from the perpendicular). The ecliptic (sun's apparent path) is inclined **23.5° to the celestial equator** = **obliquity of the ecliptic**.
- **Rotation → day and night; revolution → year; tilt (23.5°) → seasons.** Seasons are **not** due to the varying sun–earth distance.
- **Equinox** (~21 March, ~23 September): sun over the equator, day = night everywhere; maximum rate of change of day length.
- **Summer solstice** (~21 June): sun over the **Tropic of Cancer 23.5°N**, longest NH day. **Winter solstice** (~22 December): sun over the **Tropic of Capricorn 23.5°S**, shortest NH day.
- The sun can be overhead (altitude 90°) only between **23.5°N and 23.5°S**.
- **66.6° N/S** (≈ Arctic/Antarctic circle): sun does not rise on some winter days. **64.5°**: sun does not set in summer. **60.5°**: sun never more than 6° below the horizon in summer → continuous civil twilight.

### Horizon and twilight
- **Sensible horizon**: horizon through the observer without refraction (what a spirit level senses); lies **above** the visible horizon. **Visible horizon**: lowered by refraction.
- Sunrise/sunset: when the **upper edge** of the sun crosses the visible horizon; the sun's centre is then about **50′** below the sensible horizon. **Refraction makes sunrise earlier and sunset later.**
- Twilight (sun's centre below the sensible horizon): **civil 0–6°** (used in flying) · **nautical 6–12°** · **astronomical 12–18°**.
- Air almanac sunrise/sunset/twilight times are in **LMT**.

### Moon
- Shines by reflected sunlight; **synodic month ≈ 29½ days**. Rises/sets about **50 min later** each day; one day a month with no moonrise and one with no moonset. Waxing = growing, waning = shrinking, gibbous = swollen on one side, crescent = convex + concave edge.

### Year and day
- Civil year ≈ **365.25 days**; leap year every 4 years; century years are leap years only if divisible by **400** (2000 yes; 1700, 1800, 1900 no) → loses 3 days per 400 years.
- **Apparent solar day**: between two transits of the **true sun** (varies 23 h 44 min – 24 h 14 min, because the orbit is elliptical, not because rotation varies).
- **Mean solar day**: based on the **mean sun**, a fictitious sun moving at constant speed along the **celestial equator** = 24 h.
- **Sidereal day**: between two transits of a fixed star / the **First Point of Aries** = **23 h 56 min**.
- **Equation of time** = apparent solar time − mean solar time (= sundial − clock). Mean time is ahead by about **14 min near 6 February** and behind by about **16 min near 3 November**; EOT = 0 four times a year (April, June, September, December).

### Exam traps
- Seasons ← **tilt**, not distance. Different day/night lengths ← **inclination of the ecliptic to the equator**.
- Perihelion **January**, aphelion **July**. Civil twilight = **6°**.

## 2. The Earth

### Shape and size
- The earth is an **oblate spheroid (ellipsoid)**: flattened at the poles. Polar diameter ≈ **23½ NM shorter** than equatorial. For navigation it is treated as a sphere.
- **Polar radius 6 356 752 m (3432 NM)**; **equatorial radius 6 378 137 m (3443 NM)**.
- **Compression (ellipticity) ≈ 1/297** = (major axis − minor axis) / major axis. Satellite geodesy shows a slight **pear shape**, more mass in the SH.
- **Rotation**: seen from above the **North Pole anticlockwise** (west → east); from above the South Pole clockwise. The earth spins about its **polar diameter (axis)**.

### Nautical mile
- **1 NM = length of 1 minute of arc along a meridian**. Because of flattening it varies: about **6046 ft at the equator**, **6108 ft at the poles** (longer at the poles). Standard (since 1929): **1852 m = 6076.1 ft = 1.1508 statute miles**.

### Latitude
- **Latitude**: angle along the meridian from the **equator** (datum) to the place, 0–**90°** N or S.
- **Parallels of latitude**: small circles parallel to the equator (the equator is the only parallel that is a great circle); they cut every meridian at 90° → **rhumb lines**.
- **Geocentric latitude**: angle at the earth's centre. **Geodetic (geographic) latitude**: angle between the **normal** to the spheroid and the equatorial plane — used on charts. Maximum difference **~11.6′ at about 45°** N/S.

### Longitude and meridians
- **Longitude**: angle at the centre along the equator from the **prime (Greenwich) meridian** (datum) to the meridian of the place, 0–180° E or W.
- **Meridian**: semi-great circle pole to pole joining places of equal longitude; defines true north–south; crosses the equator at 90°. **Anti-meridian** = longitude 180° away, opposite name (030°W ↔ 150°E); meridian + anti-meridian = a full great circle.
- **Graticule**: network of meridians and parallels **on a map/chart** (they are imaginary lines, not marked on the earth).
- **Cardinal points**: N, S, E, W.

### Great circle, small circle, rhumb line
- **Great circle (GC)**: circle whose plane passes through the earth's **centre**, dividing it into two equal halves; radius, centre and diameter = the earth's. The **shorter arc of a GC is the shortest distance** between two points. Only one GC through two points unless they are diametrically opposite (then infinitely many). **Radio waves follow GC paths.**
- **Small circle**: any circle on the surface that is not a GC.
- **Rhumb line (loxodrome)**: cuts **all meridians at the same angle** → constant track; not the shortest distance; spirals towards the nearer pole. **Equator** is the only line that is both GC and rhumb line (meridians are GCs and also rhumb lines of 000/180 — the book counts the equator).

### Change of latitude and longitude
- **d.lat (ch.lat)**: same hemisphere → subtract; opposite hemispheres → **add** (40°N to 30°S = 70°).
- **d.long (ch.long)**: same hemisphere → subtract; opposite hemispheres → add, but if the sum exceeds 180° take **360° − sum** (go the short way across 180°). Direction: anticlockwise seen from above N pole = **east**, clockwise = **west**.
- Examples: 165°W → 103°W: d.long 62°E. 165°W → 170°E: 15° + 10° = **25°W** (across the anti-meridian). 176°W ↔ 177°E: **7°**.

### Exam traps
- Ellipticity 1/297: 6956 km equatorial axis → polar ≈ 6932 km.
- Max geocentric–geodetic difference at **45°**.
- NM longest at the **poles**.

## 3. Projections

### Why projections distort
- A sphere cannot be flattened without distortion. An ideal chart would have correct and constant **scale**, correct **shapes** and **areas**, correct **bearings**, **great circles as straight lines**, meridians and parallels crossing at **90°**, and adjacent sheets fitting together. No projection has all of these.
- **Map**: graticule plus lots of ground detail. **Chart**: graticule with little detail, made for a purpose (navigation).
- Construction: **geometric/perspective** (light source projects the graticule onto a plane, cylinder or cone: e.g. polar stereographic); **geometric but mathematically modified** (Mercator, transverse Mercator); **purely mathematical** (Lambert's conformal).
- Developable surfaces: **cylindrical** (Mercator), **conical** (Lambert), **zenithal/azimuthal** (plane; polar stereographic).

### Orthomorphism (conformality)
- **Orthomorphic / conformal** = bearings shown correctly. Needs (a) meridians and parallels crossing at **90°** and (b) **scale the same in all directions at a point**.
- Conformal: Lambert's conformal conic, Mercator, transverse Mercator, oblique Mercator, stereographic. **Not** conformal: gnomonic, orthographic, equidistant, simple cylindrical, simple conic.

### Simple cylindrical (tangent at the equator, light at centre)
- Meridians: straight, parallel, equally spaced. Parallels: straight, spacing increases towards the poles. Chart convergence **0** (earth convergence = d.long × sin lat). Rhumb line straight; GC curved concave to the equator. **Not** orthomorphic.

### Mercator (direct)
- Cylindrical, mathematically modified to be **orthomorphic**; non-perspective.
- **Meridians**: parallel, equally spaced, vertical straight lines. **Parallels**: straight, spacing increases away from the equator.
- **Scale** correct at the equator; expands as **sec lat** (scale at lat = scale at equator × sec lat; E–W and N–S expand equally). Practically constant within ~8° of the equator. Unusable near the poles (limit ~70°); the poles cannot be shown.
- **Rhumb line = straight line**. **Great circle = curve convex to the nearer pole (concave to the equator)**; meridians and equator are straight.
- **Convergence**: zero on the chart; correct only at the equator.
- Uses: rhumb-line navigation, low latitudes, **Indian met charts**. Drawbacks: radio bearings (GCs) need **conversion angle** before plotting; rhumb lines add distance on long routes.
- Distance: use the **latitude scale at the mid-latitude** of the line.

### Simple conic and Lambert's conformal conic (LCC)
- Cone tangent at a standard parallel; meridians are straight lines converging to the pole; parallels are arcs of circles.
- **LCC**: mathematically made conformal using **two standard parallels**. **Scale correct on the standard parallels**, contracts slightly between them (minimum at the **parallel of origin**, error < 1%), expands outside. The **nominal scale** is that at the standard parallels.
- **Convergence = d.long × sin (parallel of origin)**; chart convergence **correct at the parallel of origin**.
- **GC: curve concave to the parallel of origin**, treated as a **straight line**. **Rhumb line: curve concave to the pole**. Midway, the RL is parallel to the GC.
- Uses: plotting charts, topographical, **radio navigation (Jeppesen)**, airways and synoptic charts. Measure rhumb-line track with the protractor at the **mid-meridian**.

### Polar stereographic
- Zenithal: plane tangent at the **pole**, light at the **opposite pole**. The only projection that is both **perspective and orthomorphic**.
- Meridians: straight lines radiating from the pole; parallels: concentric circles, spacing increases away from the pole.
- **Convergence factor = 1** (chart convergence = d.long), correct only at the pole.
- Scale correct at the pole, expands away as **sec² (½ co-lat)** (within 1% to 78°, 3% to 70°).
- **GC ≈ straight line** near the pole (curve concave to the pole further out); **rhumb line: curve concave to the pole**. Used for **polar** navigation.

### Transverse and oblique Mercator
- **Transverse**: cylinder tangent along a chosen **meridian** and its anti-meridian. Conformal; scale correct along the **datum meridian**, expanding as sec (GC distance from it). Convergence correct at the **equator and poles**. Straight lines at 90° to the datum meridian are GCs; meridians and parallels are complex curves (parallels ellipses).
- **Oblique**: cylinder tangent along a chosen **great circle** (route). Conformal; scale correct along that GC; within ~500 NM treat as constant scale.

### Quick rules
- **GC always bows towards the pole; RL always bows towards the equator.** On Mercator the RL is straight; on Lambert and polar stereographic the GC is (nearly) straight.
- Meridians: GCs (and RLs of 000/180). Parallels (except the equator): RLs. Flying along a parallel = 090/270 T; along a meridian = 000/180 T.
- Plotting radio bearings: always from the station, magnetic → true; no convergency correction on Lambert/polar charts; **conversion angle** needed on Mercator.

### Scale
- **Scale = chart distance / earth distance**, shown as a **representative fraction** (1 : 1 000 000 — same units both sides), a **statement in words** (1 cm = 10 km) or a **graduated scale bar**. Smaller denominator = **larger scale** = more detail.
- Units: 1 NM = 1852 m ≈ 6080 ft; 1 km = 3280 ft; 1 SM = 5280 ft; 1 in = 2.54 cm; 1000 m = 0.54 NM. NM > SM > km.
- Mercator: scale at the equator = scale at lat × cos lat (denominators: equator = lat ÷ cos lat). E.g. 1 : 1 000 000 at 57°N → 1 : 1 838 235 at the equator.
- **Relief**: contours (lines of equal elevation; approximate = form lines), spot heights, layer tinting, hachuring, hill shading.

### Polar stereographic straight-line tracks (worked method)
- Draw the pole, the two points and the d.long angle at the pole; the triangle is isosceles, base angles = (180 − d.long)/2. NH: north is **towards** the pole; SH: north is **away** from it. The track is due E/W at the **mid-longitude**; total change of track = d.long (convergence factor 1).
- 70°N 060°W → 70°N 060°E: d.long 120°, initial 030°, final 150°, easterly at 000°.

## 4. Convergency

### Definitions
- **Convergency**: the angle of inclination between two meridians at a given latitude = the **change of great-circle track** between them. Zero at the equator, equal to d.long at the poles; varies as **sin latitude**.
- **Earth convergency = d.long × sin (mean) latitude** (use the mean latitude when the points are at different latitudes).
- **Conversion angle (CA)** = angle between the GC and the RL at either end = **½ convergency** (maximum near the poles).
- Example: meridians 040°W and 040°E at 30°N: convergency = 80 × sin 30 = 40°; GC track 070° at C becomes 110° at D.

### On charts
- **Mercator**: meridians parallel → chart convergence **0**; correct only at the **equator**. All RLs straight; GCs curve concave to the equator (on the **polar side** of the RL).
- **Lambert**: chart convergence = d.long × sin (parallel of origin). **Polar stereographic**: = d.long.

### Working rules
- **The GC always lies on the polar side of the RL.** RL track = GC track ± CA, applied **at the point where the GC direction is measured**.
- NH, eastbound: GC initial track is less than RL (more northerly) → RL = GC + CA at departure; at arrival RL = GC − CA. SH: the opposite. Draw a sketch every time.
- RL track is constant; at the mid-point GC track = RL track. GC track at the far end = initial GC ± convergency.
- **Which hemisphere?** From the two GC bearings (A→B and B→A): difference **= 180° → equator**; **> 180° → northern**; **< 180° → southern** (e.g. 245 − 055 = 190 → NH; 235 − 065 = 170 → SH).
- Finding d.long from a GC bearing between two points on the same parallel: RL is 090/270, so CA = GC − RL, convergency = 2 CA, d.long = convergency / sin lat. E.g. 30°N, GC 273° from A (8°E): CA 3°, conv 6°, d.long 12° → B at 4°W.
- Worked example: NH, GC A→B 040°, RL 045° → CA 5°, convergency 10°; RL B→A 225°, GC B→A 230°.

### Exam traps
- Formula is **d.long × sin mean lat** (not d.lat, not cos).
- CA is **half** the convergency.
- 60°N, 120°W → 140°30′W: RL 270°, convergency ≈ 17.8°, initial GC ≈ 279°.

## 5. Time

### Basics
- Places east see the sun earlier: **time is ahead in the east, behind in the west**, at **4 min per degree** of longitude.
- **24 h = 360°; 1 h = 15°; 1 min = 15′; 1 s = 15″**. Conversely **1° = 4 min; 1′ = 4 s**.
- **LMT (Local Mean Time)**: time on the observer's meridian measured from the mean sun's transit of the observer's **anti-meridian** (midnight = 0000). Places east are ahead, west behind.
- **GMT / UTC**: LMT of the Greenwich meridian; UTC is kept by atomic clocks (≈ GMT); the same everywhere. GD/UD = Greenwich/universal date.
- **Mean time** was introduced to have a constant measure of time independent of the sun's irregular apparent motion (book key: "one fixed time within a country" for its Q9).
- **Standard time**: time set by the legal authorities for a country or part of it, kept on a chosen meridian. **IST = UTC + 5 h 30 min (82°30′E)**; IST and LMT agree only at 82°30′E.

### Conversions
- **"Longitude east, UTC least; longitude west, UTC best"**: LMT = UTC **+** d.long/15 (east); LMT = UTC **−** d.long/15 (west).
- Problems with flight time: convert departure to **UTC**, add the flight time, convert the arrival UTC to local. **Always work in UTC.**
- Examples: UTC 1200 → LMT at 60°E 1600, at 80°W 0640. 46°25′E with LMT 14:23:15 (2 June) → UTC 11:17:35. UTC 21:02:24 2 Dec at 96°47′E → LMT 03:29:32 on 3 Dec. 95°20′W at 0000 UTC → 1738:40 the previous day.

### International Date Line
- Roughly the 180° meridian (bent around land). At 179°59′E LMT is nearly UTC + 12 h; at 179°59′W nearly UTC − 12 h: a full day apart.
- **Crossing westbound (E → W across the line, i.e. from the Americas to Asia): date goes forward one day. Crossing eastbound: date goes back one day.** (East of the line the date is one day earlier.)

### Sunrise and sunset
- Depend on the sun's declination and the observer's latitude; tabulated in the **air almanac in LMT**. On a given date, **sunrise occurs at the same LMT everywhere on the same latitude** → find the LMT, then convert to UTC for the longitude.
- E.g. sunrise at 50°N 025°E at 0254 UTC (= 0434 LMT) → at 50°N 040°W: 0434 LMT = 0714 UTC.

### Zone time
- 24 zones of 15°, lettered A–Z (except I and O). **Zone number = longitude ÷ 15** (≤ 0.5 round down, > 0.5 round up); **ZT = UTC + ZN (east +, west −)**.
- E.g. 75°E: ZN 5; ZT 0100 → UTC 2000 (previous day). 160°E at LMT 1040: ZN 11 → ZT is 20 min ahead of LMT.

### Exam traps
- Always convert to UTC first. Watch the date when crossing midnight or the date line.
- Same latitude → same LMT of sunrise/sunset.

## 6. Compass and Directions

### Three norths
- **True direction (°T)**: measured from **true north** (towards the geographic pole, the earth's spin axis). A line drawn on a map shows a **true** direction.
- **Magnetic direction (°M)**: measured from **magnetic north** — where a freely suspended magnet points (the magnetic and geographic axes are not aligned).
- **Compass direction (°C)**: measured from **compass north** — where the aircraft's compass actually points, disturbed by the aircraft's own magnetic fields.

### Variation and deviation
- **Variation (magnetic declination)**: angle between true and magnetic north. Magnetic north **east** of true = **easterly (+)**; west = **westerly (−)**. Changes from place to place (and with mineral deposits); given on maps and charts.
- **Isogonals**: lines of equal variation. **Agonic line**: zero variation.
- **Deviation**: angle between magnetic and compass north caused by metal, magnets and electrics in the aircraft. Compass north east of magnetic = **easterly (+)**, west = **westerly (−)**.
- Rhymes: **Variation east, magnetic least; variation west, magnetic best. Deviation east, compass least; deviation west, compass best.**
- Order: **C – D – M – V – T** ("Cadbury's Dairy Milk Very Tasty"): C ± D = M; M ± V = T; going C → T **add east, subtract west**.
- Example: CH 074°, deviation 4°E → MH 078°; variation 10°E → TH 088°. C 272°, D 6°W → M 266°, V 4°E → T 270°.
- Table practice (book): C 120, D 10W → M 110, V 15E → T 125. T 220, V 12W → M 232, D 12W → C 244. M 300, D 16W → C 316, V 16E → T 316.

### Exam traps
- When variation is east, the magnetic direction is **less** than the true direction.
- Agonic = zero variation; aclinic = zero dip (next chapter).

## 7. Distances on the Earth's Surface

### Units
- **Nautical mile**: arc subtending **1′ at the earth's centre** (along a great circle/meridian). Varies slightly: ~6046 ft at the equator, ~6080 ft (standard, at 45°), ~6108–6170 ft at the poles (book quotes 6170) — **longer at the poles**. **1° = 60 NM; 1′ = 1 NM.** Defined value 1852 m = 6076 ft.
- **Kilometre**: originally **1/10 000 of the meridian from equator to pole**. 1 km = 3280 ft = **0.54 NM**; 1 NM = **1.852 km**; 1 m = 3.28 ft.
- **Statute mile** = 5280 ft = **0.87 NM** (1 NM = 1.15 SM). NM > SM > km.
- International aviation units: horizontal distance in **metres, kilometres and nautical miles**; heights/altitudes in **metres and feet**.

### Great circle distance along a meridian (or the equator)
- **GC distance (NM) = d.lat in degrees × 60** (along a meridian). Over the pole (meridian + anti-meridian): (90 − lat₁) + (90 − lat₂), × 60. E.g. 56°N 070°W → 62°N 110°E = 62° = **3720 NM**.

### Departure (rhumb-line distance along a parallel)
- **Departure (NM) = d.long (minutes) × cos lat**. Maximum at the equator, zero at the poles.
- 1° of longitude = 60 × cos lat NM (30 NM at 60°N). Circumference of a parallel = 21 600 × cos lat (10 800 NM at 60°).
- Examples: 27°27′N, 030°24′E → 108°47′E: d.long 78°23′ = 4703′ × cos 27°27′ = **4173 NM**. 45°20′N, fly 800 NM on 270° from 010°05′E: d.long = 800 / 0.7029 = 1138′ = 18°58′ → **008°53′W**.
- **Find latitude from departure**: cos lat = departure / d.long′ (250 NM over 4°50′ → cos lat 0.86 → 30°41′).
- "Square" flights: N 600, E 600, S 600, W 600 do **not** close — the E and W legs are at different latitudes, so the d.long differs. A route along the higher-latitude parallel is shorter (53°N vs 60°N over 20° of longitude: 722 vs 600 NM).

### Exam traps
- Convert km → NM (÷ 1.852) before converting to degrees of latitude.
- Watch for crossing 180° when adding d.long.
- 1° of latitude is always 60 NM; 1° of longitude is 60 NM only on the equator.

## 8. Magnetism and Compasses

### Magnets
- Magnetic materials: iron, steel, nickel, cobalt, some alloys. A freely suspended magnet aligns roughly N–S; the north-seeking end is the **red (north) pole**, south-seeking the **blue (south) pole**. Like poles repel, unlike attract; force falls with the **square of distance**.

### Earth's magnetism
- The earth behaves as if a huge bar magnet sat at its centre. The magnetic pole is **not** at the true pole and slowly circles it (~960 years per orbit); now ~750 NM from the true pole, in northern Canada.
- Total field **T** splits into horizontal **H** (the **directive force**) and vertical **Z**. **tan dip = Z/H**; **T = H / cos dip**; T² = H² + Z² (units micro-tesla).
- **Dip (inclination)**: angle of a freely suspended magnet below the horizontal. **0° at the magnetic equator, 90° at the magnetic poles**. H is greatest at the magnetic equator and zero at the poles → compasses useless near the poles; a direct-reading compass is unreliable beyond ~**70°** N/S.
- **Isoclinals**: equal dip. **Aclinic line**: zero dip (magnetic equator). **Isogonals**: equal variation (converge at both the magnetic and geographic poles). **Agonic line**: zero variation. Variation at a place changes slowly over time (annual change shown on charts).
- Example: dip 40°, H = 23 → Z = 23 tan 40 ≈ 19, T = 23 / cos 40 ≈ 30.

### Aircraft magnetism
- **Hard iron** (permanent): hard to magnetise, keeps it; acquired during manufacture (hammering in the earth's field). Does not change with heading or latitude. Components **P** (fore-aft, + when blue pole ahead), **Q** (athwartships, + when blue pole to the right), **R** (vertical, ignored).
- **Soft iron** (induced, temporary): easy to magnetise, loses it; induced by the earth's field, changes with heading and latitude. **Vertical soft iron** (VSI) components **cZ** (fore-aft) and **fZ** (lateral); zero at the magnetic equator (Z = 0).
- Deviation from hard iron ∝ **1/H** (worse at high latitude); from VSI ∝ **Z/H = tan dip** (doubly worse at high latitude).

### Coefficients
- **A**: constant on all headings; misaligned lubber line (apparent A, corrected by realigning the lubber line) and horizontal soft iron (real A). **A = Σ deviations on 8+ headings / n**.
- **B** = P + cZ: deviation **max on E/W, zero on N/S**; **B = (dev E − dev W) / 2**; dev due to B = B sin hdg. Corrected on an **easterly** heading.
- **C** = Q + fZ: deviation **max on N/S, zero on E/W**; **C = (dev N − dev S) / 2**; dev due to C = C cos hdg. Corrected on a **northerly** heading.
- **Total deviation = A + B sin hdg + C cos hdg**. Max deviation where tan hdg = B/C; zero where tan hdg = −C/B. Residual deviation goes on a **deviation (compass) card**.
- Example (book): dev N +7, S +1, E 0, W +4 → B = −2, C = +3; max-deviation heading ≈ 326°, zero ≈ 304°, deviation at 326° ≈ 3.6°E. A +3, B +2, C −10 → 9°E on 222°C, 12°E on 146°C.

### Compass swing
- Aligning compass north with **magnetic north** (finding and reducing deviation), using a datum such as a **medium landing compass** (portable, tripod, prism sight).
- Swing: new aircraft; new compass; **every 3 months** (book); after major inspection; change of magnetic material; large change of latitude/base; after **lightning strike** or flying in static; after standing on one heading **> 4 weeks**; carrying magnetic freight; for a C of A; after a heavy landing; whenever deviation is suspect.
- Siting limits: no single item may cause > 1° deviation; all items together ≤ 2° (non-electrical and electrical separately).

### Errors due to dip (direct-reading compass)
- The magnet's low centre of gravity counters dip, which causes **acceleration** and **turning** errors (none at the magnetic equator).
- **Acceleration/deceleration**: max on **E/W** headings, nil on N/S. **APDE**: Acceleration → apparent turn towards the nearer **Pole**; Deceleration → towards the **Equator**. NH: accelerating on west, card turns clockwise, under-reads, shows a turn to the right/north.
- **Turning errors**: max turning onto/through **N/S**, nil on E/W. **Turning through the nearer pole the compass is sluggish (undershoot) → roll out early; through the equator it is lively (overshoot) → roll out late.** ("APPS")
- Rule of thumb: roll out early/late by about **latitude ± ½ bank** before N/S (e.g. 45°N, 16° bank: from east to north roll out at 037°).
- **Liquid swirl**: fluid dragged round in turns carries the magnets in the direction of turn; adds to or reduces turning error; at the equator it is the only turning error.
- Read the compass only in **straight and level, unaccelerated** flight.

### Direct reading compass (E-type)
- Mandatory standby: a direct reading **non-stabilised** compass. Parts: card, magnets, lubber line, fluid, expansion unit, compensating magnets/screws, lamp. **Pendulously** mounted magnet system with low magnetic moment.

### Requirements of a direct-reading compass (p.100–101)
- **Horizontality**: the card stays level away from the magnetic equator: magnets hung **below the pivot** (their weight opposes Z) and the pivot moved towards the nearer pole.
- **Aperiodicity (dead beat)**: settles without oscillating: bowl filled with **methyl alcohol or silicone fluid** plus **damping filaments**; several **short powerful magnets** instead of one big one (more righting force, less inertia); weight kept near the pivot and reduced by the liquid's buoyancy.
- **Sensitivity**: stays on the magnetic meridian: **strong magnets**, minimum pivot friction (**iridium** pivot), liquid that lightens the system and lubricates the pivot.

### DRC serviceability tests (p.101)
- Liquid free of **bubbles, discoloration, sediment**; all parts **luminous**; grid ring turns freely through 360° and its lock works; bowl suspension moves freely with no metal-to-metal feel.
- **Pivot friction**: deflect the magnets **10–15° each way**; the two return readings within **2°** of each other.
- **Damping**: deflect **90°**, hold **30 s**, time the return through **85°**: usually about **6.5–8.5 s** (limits in the instrument manual).

### Remote indicating (gyro-magnetic / slaved) compass
- Combines the **north-seeking** ability of the magnetic compass with the **stability of a gyro (DI)**. A DI drifts and must be reset every 10–15 min; the slaved compass does it automatically.
- **Detector unit (flux valve)**: three flux valves at **120°**, pendulously mounted to stay horizontal, fixed in azimuth, mounted at a **wing tip or fin top** away from aircraft magnetism. It **senses** (not seeks) the magnetic meridian → works to higher latitudes.
- Signal to a **selsyn** whose null-seeking rotor drives the card; precession (slaving) slow (~5°/min); **roll cut-out** above ~10° bank reduces turning error. Can be used in DG (free) mode; feeds RMI, autopilot etc.
- Disadvantages: heavier, more expensive, electrical (can fail), complex.

### Exam traps
- Coefficient **B on E/W**, **C on N/S**. Correct B on east, C on north.
- APDE and "sluggish through the pole".
- Dip zero at the magnetic equator; H max there.

## 9. Dead Reckoning (DR) Navigation

### Terms
- **DR**: navigating by computation from airspeed, heading, wind and time alone. Errors accumulate with time (INS is also a DR system).
- **Track (TR)**: path over the ground, normally °T. **Required track (RT)**: wanted; **Track made good (TMG)**: actually flown (join the start and a fix).
- **Heading (HDG)**: where the nose points (°C/M/T/Grid). **Course**: intended direction of flight (US usage: course = required track, track = actual path).
- **W/V**: direction the wind blows **from** / speed, e.g. 330/25.
- Airspeeds: **ASIR** (raw) → **IAS** (ASI calibrated for ISA MSL compressible flow) → **CAS** (corrected for position & instrument error; = TAS at MSL ISA; UK: RAS) → **EAS** (corrected for compressibility; matters above ~300 kt TAS) → **TAS** (corrected for density). **Mach = TAS / LSS**.
- **GS** = TAS ± wind component. **ETE** = distance ÷ GS. **ETA** (IFR: over the IAF/nav aid; VFR: over the aerodrome).
- **Fix**: accurate position from visual, radio or radar means. DR circle of error ≈ **5% of TAS**-ish per the book's rule of thumb; Doppler DR error ellipse ≈ 0.1% along / 0.2% across distance since the fix.

### Triangle of velocities
- Six quantities: **HDG/TAS (one arrow)**, **W/V (three arrows)**, **TR/GS (two arrows)**. Know four → find two. Air vector + wind vector = ground vector.
- **Drift**: angle from heading to track. Track right of heading = **starboard (S)**; left = **port (P)**. **TR = HDG + S drift − P drift.** WCA = drift applied the other way (drift 7°S ⇒ WCA 7°P).
- **Track error (TE)**: angle from required track to TMG; starboard/port likewise; **TMG = RT ± TE**.
- Beam winds: wind 90° to **track** → GS < TAS; wind 90° to **heading** → GS > TAS.

### Runway components
- Angle θ = wind direction (°M) − runway (°M). **Headwind = W cos θ**, **crosswind = W sin θ** (negative cos → tailwind). Convert a true wind to magnetic first (runways are magnetic).
- Quick fractions: 30° → ½ crosswind; 45° → 0.7; 60° → 0.9.
- Example: W/V 090°T/40, var 10°W → 100°M/40; runway 07: θ 30°, headwind 35 kt, crosswind 20 kt from the right; on runway 25 a 35 kt tailwind.

### Time and DR position
- **Time = distance ÷ GS.** Mean wind over several legs: weight components by time.
- Revised ETA after a fix: use the GS actually achieved. Example: 120 NM in 20 min = 360 kt.
- **Rate of descent** = height to lose ÷ time (e.g. FL290 → FL80 over 50 NM at 243 kt: 21 000 ft in 12.3 min ≈ 1700 ft/min).
- Losing time: cutting/extending a corner, a **60° dog-leg** (alter 60°, then 120° back: an equilateral triangle; the time lost equals one side), or changing airspeed.
- **Lost procedure**: climb (range of radios/radar), use VOR/ADF/GPS, contact ATC (DF, radar), emergency **121.5 MHz**, squawk **7700**.

### Exam traps
- Starboard drift is **subtracted** from track to get heading; port drift added.
- Convert true wind to magnetic before runway components.

## 10. Measurement of DR Elements: Pressure (and Altimetry)

### ISA and density
- **ISA**: MSL **15 °C**, **1013.25 hPa** (29.92 inHg, 760 mm, 14.7 lb/in²), density **1.225 kg/m³**; lapse **1.98 °C/1000 ft** to **36 090 ft**, then **−56.5 °C** to ~65 600 ft. (Jet standard: 2 °C/1000 ft.)
- **Density ∝ pressure; ∝ 1/temperature; ∝ 1/humidity** (moist air is lighter). Hot, humid, high = low density = poor performance.
- With height both pressure and temperature fall; the pressure effect dominates, so density falls. Pressure falls **exponentially**.
- ISA pressure–height: **1013 hPa MSL · 700 → 10 000 ft · 500 → 18 000 ft · 300 → 30 000 ft · 200 → 38 000 ft · 100 → 53 000 ft**.
- **Feet per hPa**: **27 ft at MSL**, 34 ft at 10 000 ft, 50 ft at 18 000 ft, 100 ft at 40 000 ft (book: 30 ft used in many sums).
- ISA at 18 000 ft: −20.7 °C, 506 hPa.

### Altimeter settings and altitudes
- **QFE**: pressure at the aerodrome datum → altimeter reads **height** above the aerodrome/threshold.
- **QNH**: QFE reduced to MSL **using ISA** → reads **altitude** (AMSL); shows elevation on the ground.
- **QFF**: QFE reduced to MSL using the **actual** temperature (met charts).
- **Standard setting 1013.25 → pressure altitude / flight level**. PA is used for density altitude, true altitude, TAS, performance and separation.
- **PA = elevation + (1013 − QNH) × 30** (e.g. 4000 ft, QNH 1025 → 3640 ft; 1000 ft, QNH 1025 → 640 ft; QFE 1022 → −270 ft).
- **True altitude** (temperature correction): **4% of indicated altitude per 10 °C** off ISA (warmer → higher). E.g. 20 000 ft, OAT −10 °C (ISA −25, dev +15) → 21 200 ft; 17 000 ft at −30 °C (ISA −19, dev −11) → 16 252 ft.
- **Density altitude** = PA corrected for non-ISA temperature ≈ **PA + 118–120 ft × ISA deviation** (e.g. PA 10 000, +12 °C → ≈ 12 000 ft; PA 0, 10 °C → −593 ft). DA is the PA at which the prevailing **density** occurs in ISA.
- Take-off and landing performance: based on **actual** temperatures (book key).
- Altimeter problems: build a small table of setting / QNH / elevation / indication; each hPa difference = 30 ft; **high to low (or hot to cold) → altimeter over-reads**. E.g. altimeter on 1013 landing where QNH 1008, elevation 1240 → reads 1375 ft.

### 1-in-60 rule
- 1° off over 60 NM = 1 NM off. **Track error = off-track × 60 / distance gone; closing angle = off × 60 / distance to go.** Valid for angles up to ~20° (tan θ ≈ θ/60).
- Vertical use: **height (ft) ≈ distance (NM) × angle (°) × 101** (6080/60). 3° glide path: ~318 ft per NM; **ROD ≈ GS (kt) × angle × 1.69** (≈ GS × 5 for 3°).
- Radar cloud height: from tilt angle and range by the same rule (half beam width matters).

### ILS deflection
- Full-scale: **glide slope ±0.7°**, **localiser ±2.5°**. 5-dot display: each dot **0.14°** (GS) / **0.5°** (LOC); 4-dot: 0.175° / 0.625°.
- Max safe glide-slope deflection: **half scale**; beyond → missed approach.
- Example: 3 NM, 2 dots fly-up (0.28°): 3 × 0.28 × 101 ≈ 85 ft below the GP; 2 dots fly-right (1°) → 303 ft (≈ 92 m) left.

### Exam traps
- QNH uses **ISA**; QFF uses actual temperature.
- DA rises with temperature; TA = PA corrected by 4%/10 °C.

## 11. Measurement of Temperature (and Airspeed)

### SAT and TAT
- **SAT / OAT / true air temperature / ambient**: the temperature of the undisturbed air.
- Moving air brought to rest at the probe heats adiabatically: **ram rise**. The probe reads **TAT** (total / impact / ram / indicated air temperature), always **warmer than SAT**, by an amount that grows with **TAS** (∝ TAS²).
- **TAT = SAT (1 + 0.2 × r × M²)** in kelvin (r = recovery factor, 1 if unknown); **SAT = TAT ÷ (1 + 0.2 M²)**. Example: TAT −9 °C (264 K), M0.86 → SAT 230 K = −43 °C (ISA at FL310 −47 → ISA +4).
- Simple form: **ram rise ≈ (TAS/87.1)²** °C (sometimes (TAS/100)²). E.g. TAS 520 kt → rise ≈ 35.6 °C.
- **Recovery factor**: fraction of the full ram rise a probe senses (0.75 sensing 5 °C → full rise 6.7 °C).
- TAT is used by the FMC for thrust/engine performance; SAT is reported in position reports.
- **Air Data Computer (ADC)** inputs: static pressure, total (pitot) pressure, TAT, AoA, power. Outputs: altitude (from **static pressure**), IAS/CAS, Mach, TAS, SAT, density; it turns air data into electrical signals for the instruments.

### Airspeeds
- **ASIR → (instrument error) → IAS → (position/pressure error) → CAS (= RAS) → (compressibility) → EAS → (density) → TAS**; TAS ± wind = GS.
- At constant CAS: higher altitude or temperature → lower density → **higher TAS**. Lift-off always at the same **CAS** (EAS).
- Rule of thumb: **TAS ≈ RAS + 1.75 × RAS × FL/1000** (book form), or ≈ CAS + 2% per 1000 ft.

### Mach number
- **M = TAS / LSS**; **LSS = 38.94 √T(K) kt** (book: 39 √T; 661.5 kt at 15 °C). LSS depends **only on temperature**.
- For the same TAS, Mach rises with altitude (colder); for the same Mach, TAS falls with altitude (to the tropopause). The Machmeter has no compressibility error.
- Examples: −40 °C → LSS 596 kt; −56.5 °C → 573 kt; M0.86 at −42 °C → LSS 593 kt, TAS 510 kt. A change of 0.15 M for 93 kt → LSS 620 kt.

### Climbs and descents (book's graphs)
- **ISA layer (below the tropopause)**: climbing at **constant CAS** → TAS ↑ and Mach ↑; at **constant Mach** → TAS ↓ and CAS ↓; at **constant TAS** → CAS ↓, Mach ↑.
- **Isothermal layer**: LSS constant, so constant Mach = constant TAS; climbing at constant CAS → TAS ↑, Mach ↑ (CAS falls at constant TAS/Mach).
- **Inversion (warmer aloft)**: climbing at constant TAS → Mach ↓ (LSS ↑), CAS ↓; at constant Mach → TAS ↑, CAS ↓; at constant CAS → **TAS ↑** (Mach changes little, since LSS also rises).
- Order of the lines on the book's diagrams (left → right): climbing ISA/isothermal **C-T-M** ("Coffee, Tea or Me"); descending **M-T-C**; climbing an inversion **C-M-T**; descending an inversion **T-M-C**.

### Exam traps
- TAT > SAT always; difference ∝ TAS (not CAS).
- Use kelvin in the TAT formula.
- In an isothermal layer constant Mach ⇔ constant TAS.

## 12. Measurement of Elements: PNR, PET and Range

### Point of no return (PNR / PSR / radius of action)
- The furthest point an aircraft can fly out and still return to the **departure** aerodrome within its **safe endurance**. Depends purely on **fuel** (endurance).
- **Safe endurance E = (fuel on board − reserve) ÷ fuel consumption per hour.**
- **Distance to PNR = E × O × H ÷ (O + H)** (O = GS out, H = GS home). **Time to PNR = E × H ÷ (O + H)**.
- Normally all engines operating unless stated. If TAS or fuel flow changes en route, use the **gross fuel consumption** method: GFC (units per ground NM) = FC/GS; **PNR = fuel available ÷ (GFC out + GFC home)**, or the unitary (proportion) method.
- Properties: PNR distance changes in **proportion to fuel** (10% more fuel → 10% further) and inversely with fuel consumption. **Maximum in still air; any wind reduces it.**
- **SAR (still air range)** = fuel available × TAS ÷ FC.

### Critical point (CP) / equal-time point (ETP / PET)
- Point from which it takes **equal time** to continue to the destination or return to the departure (or a designated) point. **Not** the mid-point.
- **Distance to CP = D × H ÷ (O + H)** (O, H = groundspeeds from the CP on and home; for engine failure use the **reduced TAS**).
- **Time to CP = distance ÷ GS out with all engines (normal TAS)**.
- CP is half way when O = H: **nil wind or beam wind**. **Headwind out → CP beyond half way; tailwind out → before half way. The CP moves into wind.** Same headwind but lower TAS → CP further.
- A stronger beam wind: distance unchanged (half way), **time to it increases**. Reversing a head/tail component moves the CP the same amount the other side of half way (e.g. 750 → 450 NM on 1200).
- Fuel changes do **not** move the CP. If the PNR lies before the CP, the fuel is not enough to reach the destination.

### Exam traps
- CP formula uses **D**, PNR formula uses **E** (endurance) — both × H/(O + H).
- Time to CP uses the normal all-engines GS.

## 13. In-flight Navigation

### Pre-flight planning
- Plan to fix position: **immediately after setting heading** (departure point and time for ETAs), **at regular points en route** (track and time corrections), and **at a final point** near the destination.
- Chart preparation: **time/distance markers** along the track (distance makes 1-in-60 easier); **track-error lines** at 5° or 10° either side through departure and destination; fold charts for the whole track without re-folding, number them, keep an emergency set handy.
- Planning sequence: review information (rules, NOTAM/nav warnings) → met (winds/temps) → choose charts → choose the route (aim, rules, weather, nav aids) → draw tracks, measure track and distance, log them → safe altitudes and cruise level → RAS per stage, then TAS → headings → GS and fuel → mental re-check → prepare charts → alternates and their data.

### Visual navigation (map reading)
- Larger scale = more detail; the worse the weather, the larger the scale of map to use.
- **Visual checkpoint**: big enough to see, small enough to pinpoint, **unique**. Coastlines (inlets, piers better than straight beaches), rivers/lakes (change with seasons, floods, drought, snow), hills (look flatter from higher; may be in cloud), villages (cross-check), towns (shape + roads/railways), cities (too large to fix accurately), railways and roads (line features: along track for tracking, across track for timing; junctions and bridges for pinpoints), masts (good from low levels and at a distance).
- Near objects are identified by plan shape; distant ones by elevation. Small/narrow features give the most accurate fix. Contrast, colour, sun position and season change appearances.
- In poor weather navigate **ground to map**; in good weather map to ground. Choose checkpoints **5–10 min** apart. Fix: estimate range and bearing from a checkpoint, note the time, then correct heading and speed.

### Climb, descent and cruise
- Climbs and descents are worked by DR: use the **mean TAS and mean wind at the half-way height**.
- Airway centrelines are in °M; route forecast winds in °T — convert to one reference (along airways convert the wind to °M).

### Fixes and position lines
- **Fix**: aircraft's exact position at a time, from visual, radio or radar observations (simultaneous or transferred to a common time).
- Best fix: most accurate aids, position lines cutting at **60–90°** — a **twin-DME fix** is the most accurate. With three position lines aim for **120°** cuts; their triangle is the **cocked hat** (the true fix lies at its centre).
- **ADF on an RBI**: needle shows relative bearing; **QDM = relative bearing + magnetic heading**; QDR = QDM ± 180; QTE = QDR ± variation **at the aircraft**.
- **RMI**: compass card slaved to the gyro-magnetic compass; the needle head reads the **QDM** directly.
- **VOR**: on an RMI or an **OBI** (TO flag = QDM, FROM flag = QDR); convert with variation **at the station** (constant for that VOR); plot as QTE from the station's true meridian.
- **DME**: arc of range around the station (slant range normally ignored). **TACAN** = military DME; **VORTAC** = VOR + TACAN co-located.
- A position line along track gives **tracking** info; one abeam gives **timing**. Bearings from one station at different times give a **running fix** (time = time of the last line).

### 1-in-60 corrections
- **Track error angle = off × 60 / distance along; closing angle = off × 60 / distance to go; total correction = TEA + CA** (round drift up). E.g. 600 NM gone, 300 to go, 20 NM off → 2° + 4° = 6°.
- **Time to station** (wing-tip or relative-bearing change method): **time (min) = elapsed min × 60 ÷ degrees of change**; distance = TAS × time. A **45°** change (e.g. 045 → 090 relative) means distance flown = distance to the beacon. At 60 NM, 1° of radial ≈ **1 NM**.
- Rate of climb/descent = TAS × sin(path angle); ROD (ft/min) ≈ GS × gradient% × 1.01 (≈ 101.3 ft/min per kt per 100%).

### Exam traps
- QTE from an NDB uses **variation at the aircraft**; from a VOR, **variation at the station**.
- Revised ETAs: use the GS actually achieved on the last leg.

## 14. Mass and Balance — Aeroplanes

### Definitions
- Loading, mass and CG must comply with the **Aircraft Flight Manual**; the **commander** is responsible for satisfying himself of this.
- **Datum**: point on the longitudinal axis (or its extension) from which all arms are measured (set by the manufacturer; may be ahead of the nose; small helicopters often 100 in ahead of the rotor mast to keep arms positive). Aft of datum = **positive arm**, forward = negative.
- **Arm (balance arm)**: distance from the datum to a mass's CG. **Moment = mass × arm**. **CG = total moment ÷ total mass**.
- **CG**: point through which the weight acts (vertically, along the gravity vector); point of balance; the point the aircraft manoeuvres about and the three axes pass through. Must stay within **forward and aft (and lateral) limits** — the CG range/envelope.
- **Loading index (LI)**: scaled-down moment; **DOI**: index at the DOM. **Ballast**: removable or fixed mass to bring the CG into range.
- **MAC (mean aerodynamic chord)**: chord of an equivalent rectangular wing; CG often given as **% MAC** (LeMAC/TeMAC give its position). For longitudinal stability the CG must be **ahead of the centre of lift**.
- Mass is the amount of matter; weight = mass × g (used interchangeably here).
- Conversions: lb × 0.454 = kg; kg × 2.205 = lb; Imp gal × 4.546 = L; US gal × 3.785 = L; ft × 0.305 = m; NM × 1852 = m.
- Weighing example: nose 1000 lb at −25 in, mains 2 × 2000 lb at +35 in → moment 115 000 lb·in, CG **+23 in**.

### Weighing and records (India)
- Every aircraft weighed **before issue of the C of A** (imported: manufacturer's/previous operator's schedule acceptable). **AUW > 2000 kg: re-weigh every 5 years**; **< 2000 kg: no routine re-weighing**; any aircraft after major repair/alteration or interior change affecting W&B (DGCA's decision final); DGCA may order re-weighing at any time.
- Weight schedule prepared by an appropriately licensed AME (Cat B) or approved person, approved by the **Regional Airworthiness Office**, **displayed in the aircraft**, kept with the aircraft documents until replaced.
- **PIC** ensures correct total load, distribution and lashing so the CG stays within limits at take-off, en route and landing. Scheduled operators may delegate the computation to a trained, authorised person who signs it; the PIC scrutinises and signs. Non-direct CG methods need Regional Airworthiness approval.
- **Load and trim sheet** for every flight (CAR Sec 2 Series F Part XXI trained persons), signed by the PIC or the preparer and checked by the PIC; one copy on board, one retained **at least 4 months**. It shows aircraft, fuel and oil, cargo/baggage, passengers, crew masses; the maximum allowable mass (lowest of runway-limited TOM, en-route-limited, structural landing and landing-distance-limited masses); computed mass; evidence CG is within limits; passenger names with male/female/child/infant breakdown.
- **Standard masses**: **crew 85 kg** (75 + 10), **adult 75 kg**, **child (2–12) 35 kg**, **infant (< 2) 10 kg** (training flights may use actual crew masses). Scales calibrated as required.
- Safe loading: allot seats; respect floor-loading limits; secure the load at the tie-down points; in mixed passenger/cargo cabins put cargo **ahead of** passengers without blocking emergency exits. Scheduled operators keep a W&B manual and a DGCA-approved load/trim sheet procedure.

### CG position effects
- **Forward CG**: more stable, heavier elevator, larger tail download → more induced drag, higher stall speed, more fuel, **less range/endurance**, slow pitch response.
- **Aft CG**: less stable, lighter controls, lower stall speed but risk of stall/spin (flat spin) recovery problems, overstress risk; rapid rotation on take-off; range and endurance reduced if grossly outside; glide hard to sustain.
- **Neutrally stable**: CG at or near the **aerodynamic centre (neutral point)**; no restoring tendency.
- Longitudinal stability set by the **CG relative to the neutral point**.

### Effects of increased mass
- Longer take-off and landing; higher V1, VR, V2 and stopping distance; lower climb gradient, rate and ceiling; higher stall speed, lower max speed; narrower buffet margins; more drag and fuel; less range and endurance; higher wing-root stress, gear loads, tyre and brake wear; reduced manoeuvrability. At a given IAS a heavier aeroplane flies at a higher angle of attack.

### Mass terms
- **Basic empty mass (BEM)**: aeroplane + unusable fuel and fluids, engine/APU oil, fire extinguishers, pyrotechnics, emergency oxygen, supplementary electronics (from the latest weighing schedule).
- **Dry operating mass (DOM / BOM / APS)**: ready for a specific operation **excluding usable fuel and traffic load** — includes crew and their baggage, catering and removable passenger service equipment, potable water and lavatory chemicals, food and beverages.
- **Operating mass (OM)** = DOM + take-off fuel. **Zero fuel mass (ZFM)** = DOM + traffic load. **Take-off mass (TOM)** at the start of the take-off run. **Ramp/taxi mass** = TOM + taxi fuel.
- **Traffic load (payload)**: passengers, baggage, cargo (incl. non-revenue). **Useful load = traffic load + usable fuel = TOM − DOM**.
- Fuel terms: **FOB** (trip + contingency + alternate + reserve); ramp/block fuel = FOB + taxi; **flight/trip fuel** = fuel burned en route.
- **Structural limits** (manufacturer): MSTOM, MSLM, max structural taxi mass, **MZFM** (limited by wing-root bending moment when the wing tanks are empty; above it, extra load may only be fuel).
- **Performance limits** (pilot): PLTOM (departure aerodrome) and PLLM (arrival). **Regulated (max) TOM** = lower of structural and performance TOM; same for landing.
- **Allowed TOM = least of: MZFM + take-off fuel; regulated TOM; regulated landing mass + trip fuel.** Then **maximum traffic load = allowed TOM − DOM − take-off fuel**.
- Turbojet fuel = SFC × thrust × time.
- Floor loading: pallet mass (incl. pallet and tie-downs) ÷ area ≤ floor limit; also running-metre, compartment and seating limits.

### Exam traps
- ZFM and DOM differ by the **traffic load**; useful load = traffic load + usable fuel.
- CG shift = mass moved × distance ÷ total mass.
- Fuel in litres entered as kg makes the aircraft lighter than calculated (density < 1).

## 15. Performance

### Terms
- **Pressure altitude** (chart altitude): PA with 1013 set. **Elevation**: height of an object above MSL. **Height**: from the lowest part of the aeroplane to a datum.
- **Angle of climb**: between horizontal and flight path. **Angle of attack**: between relative airflow and chord. **Climb gradient** = height gained ÷ horizontal distance × 100%.
- **Gross performance**: average fleet performance when well maintained and flown to the book. **Net performance** = gross reduced for contingencies (technique, below-average aircraft); it is improbable that net performance won't be achieved. **Gross height** used for PA in obstacle calculations and flap-retraction height; **net height/flight path** must clear obstacles by the statutory margin.
- **Induced drag** ∝ 1/V² (other drag ∝ V²): < 10% of drag in cruise/descent, ≥ 20% in climb, up to **70%** just after take-off; increases with weight.
- **Screen height**: imaginary screen at the end of TODR and start of LDR (35 ft). PMC = power management computer; QRH = quick reference handbook.
- Equations: T = D + W sin γ (thrust required); L = W cos γ; **sin(climb angle) ≈ (T − D)/W**; F = m·a; **L = ½ C_L ρ A V²**. C_L rises with angle of attack up to the critical angle, then falls (stall); increased by flaps, slats, boundary-layer control.

### Take-off flight path and segments
- Begins at **35 ft** and ends at the higher of **1500 ft** or the point where the single-engine en-route climb speed is reached. Each flap/slat retraction needs an acceleration (a level segment with one engine out).
- Climb gradient requirements assume **one engine inoperative**. **Net gradient = actual OEI gradient − 0.8%**. Net path must clear obstacles by **35 ft vertically** (200 ft laterally inside the aerodrome boundary, 300 ft outside). A standard departure needing **200 ft/NM** ≈ **3.3%**.
- **Segment 1**: from 35 ft at V2 to gear fully retracted. **Segment 2**: gear up to at least **400 ft** at V2 — **the performance-limiting segment**. **Segment 3 (transition)**: level acceleration for flap/slat retraction. **Final segment**: to at least 1500 ft, clean, maximum continuous power, final climb speed.

### V-speeds
- **V1** decision speed: max speed for the first stopping action within ASDA; min speed after failure of the critical engine (at VEF) to continue and reach screen height within TODA. **VMCG ≤ V1 ≤ VR, ≤ VMBE**.
- **VR** rotation (≥ 1.05 VMCA; rotating early = tail strike risk), **VLOF** lift-off, **V2** take-off safety speed (≥ 1.1 VMCA and ≥ 1.2 V_S / 1.13 V_SR), **V2min**, **VFTO** final take-off speed, **VEF** engine failure speed.
- **VMCA** min control speed in the air (full rudder + up to **5° bank** towards the live engine; highest with an **aft CG**); **VMCG** on the ground (≤ 667 N / 150 lbf rudder force, ≤ 30 ft deviation, no nosewheel steering; always < VMCA); **VMCL** landing configuration.
- **VA** manoeuvring (rises with mass), **VB** max gust intensity, **VC** design cruise, **VD/VDF** dive, **VFE** max flaps extended, **VLE** max gear extended, **VLO** max gear operating, **VMO/MMO** max operating, **VNE** never exceed, **VNO** max structural cruise (exceed only with caution, in smooth air), **VMBE** max brake energy, **VMU** minimum unstick, **VS/VSO/VS1/VSR** stall speeds (VSO landing configuration), **VSW** stall warning, **VX** best angle (height in shortest distance), **VY** best rate (height in shortest time), **VYSE** best rate one engine out, **VREF/VAT** threshold speed = **1.3 VSO** (or 1.23 VSR).
- Order: **VMCG ≤ V1 ≤ VR (≥ 1.05 VMCA) ≤ VLOF ≤ V2**.

### Cruise, ceiling, descent, glide
- Economy (NM per kg) improves as the aircraft gets lighter; jets cruise at constant Mach; **step climbs** approximate the ideal cruise-climb. Temperature has little net effect on kg/NM at constant Mach (higher TAS but more fuel flow).
- **Absolute ceiling**: rate of climb zero; **service ceiling**: ~100 ft/min.
- Descent: top of descent typically **80–120 NM** out, idle thrust; typical ROD 2600 ft/min at TOD → 700 ft/min low down; gear and flaps below ~5000 ft.
- **Glide**: range set by **L/D**; best glide speed at **max L/D**; weight changes only the glide **time** (and best-glide speed), not the ratio; wind changes ground distance.

### Declared distances
- **TORA**: runway available for the ground run. **TODA** = TORA + **clearway**. **ASDA** = TORA + **stopway**. **LDA**: runway for landing, reduced by a **displaced threshold** (affects only landings towards that threshold).
- **Stopway**: prepared area beyond TORA for stopping after a rejected take-off; **at least as wide as the runway**, able to bear the aircraft. Counts only in **ASDA**.
- **Clearway**: area beyond the runway (under aerodrome control) over which the initial climb may be made; **≤ half TORA** long; at least **75 m** each side of the extended centre line (instrument runway); up-slope ≤ 1.25%; obstacle-free; may include the stopway; within the airport boundary; its centre line is **imaginary**, shown on diagrams/charts. Counts in **TODA**.
- "NU" = not usable.
- Pilot: be airborne before the end of TORA; reach 35 ft before the end of TODA (even with an engine failure); stop within ASDA if rejecting before V1.
- **Balanced field length**: V1 chosen so that **ASDR = TODR**; the **shortest** field length for given conditions, allowing the **maximum take-off mass**. Raising V1 lowers TODR and raises ASDR.
- **Aeroplane reference field length**: minimum length at MTOM, sea level, ISA, nil wind, zero slope.

### Factors
- Higher pressure (density) → shorter take-off/landing, better climb. Higher temperature or altitude → longer distances, lower field- and climb-limited TOMs. Headwind → shorter distances (higher climb gradient); tailwind does not change the air-mass climb-limited TOM.
- Higher flap (to the optimum) → shorter ground roll but poorer climb; low flap suits high/hot airfields, long runways and distant obstacles. Climb-limited TOM rises with low flap, low PA, low OAT.
- Inoperative antiskid → longer accelerate-stop distance. First action on rejecting below V1: **reduce thrust**.
- Tyre speed limit applies to the **lift-off groundspeed**.

### Exam traps
- Stopway → ASDA only; clearway → TODA only; displaced threshold → LDA only.
- The **second segment** limits performance.
- VAT = 1.3 VSO (100 kt stall → 130 kt).

## 16. Flight Planning and Monitoring — Aeroplanes
*ICAO Annex 2 (Rules of the Air), Annex 6 Part 1 and Doc 4444 (Air Traffic Management).*

### Why plan (p.277–278)
- Before any commercial flight a **comprehensive flight plan** is made: **headings (M) to steer, time on each leg, fuel to be consumed, height to fly, alternates available** and other useful details. It is a guide; its **main purpose is safety**, mainly ensuring **sufficient fuel plus a bit extra for contingencies**.
- In the air, headings and times are amended, with a continuous check on **fuel consumption** and **weather ahead**.
- First step at the field: **latest Met information** for the route and all aerodromes likely to be used (forecast **W/V and temperatures** at the relevant heights) → fill in the plan → order fuel → enter range and **PNR** → report the complete plan to **ATC** for surveillance in flight.
- Forecast temperatures and W/Vs, fuel consumption and ETAs will change in flight, but the plan is the reference.

### Principles of fuel planning (p.278)
- With the information before flight, resolve it on the **computer** into the essentials of the flight.
- For fuel, **aircraft weight is the governing factor**; it falls rapidly as fuel burns, so a **mean consumption or mean TAS per sector** is used.
- **First step: deduce the temperature deviation for each stage**; check that the performance table used is for the correct **temperature-deviation range**.
- Arithmetic errors carry heavy penalties (e.g. uplifting **1000 kg too little** fuel).
- Keep a **running record of aircraft weight**.
- **Climb**: find the distance gone in the climb; subtract from the stage length → distance left in **cruise**.
- **Level**: keep watching the **temp deviation and FL**.
- **Descent**: work out the descent path → **time and distance**.
- **Other factors**: alternate, **diversion and hold**, **circuit and landing**, **reserve**. A diversion is assumed **after an unsuccessful landing attempt**, so diversion tables already include **overshoot, climb to a suitable level and reserve fuel**.

### EDTO / ETOPS (Annex 6)
- An aeroplane with **two or more turbine engines** shall not fly a route where the **diversion time to an en-route alternate** from any point exceeds the **threshold time** set by the State.
- Diversion time is calculated in **ISA and still air**: at the **one-engine-inoperative cruise speed** for **twins**, and at the **all-engines-operating cruise speed** for aeroplanes with **more than two** engines.
- **ETOPS has been replaced by EDTO** in most ICAO documents. EDTO covers **twins (ETOPS), three-engine** and **four-engine (LROPS, Long Range Operations)** aircraft.

### Definitions (Annex 2 / Doc 4444)
- **Flight plan**: specified information given to **ATS units** about an intended flight or part of a flight.
- **Filed flight plan**: as filed by the pilot or representative, **without subsequent changes**.
- **Current flight plan**: **including changes** brought about by subsequent clearances.
- **Repetitive flight plan (RPL)**: for a series of **frequently recurring, regularly operated IFR flights** with identical basic features, submitted by an operator for **retention and repetitive use** by ATS.
- **EET (estimated elapsed time)**: estimated time from **one significant point to another**.
- **EOBT (estimated off-block time)**: when the aircraft will **start moving for departure**.
- **ETA**: **IFR** — time over the **designated navaid point from which the instrument approach will start** (or over the aerodrome if no navaid); **VFR** — time over the **aerodrome**.
- **TEET (total estimated elapsed time)**: **IFR** — from **take-off** to that approach point (or over the destination aerodrome if no navaid); **VFR** — from take-off to over the **destination aerodrome**.
- **EAT (expected approach time)**: when ATC expects an aircraft that has been **delayed** to **leave the holding fix** to complete its approach or landing.
- **Alternate aerodrome**: where an aircraft may go when it is **impossible or inadvisable** to proceed to or land at the intended aerodrome:
  - **Take-off alternate**: to land **shortly after take-off** if the departure aerodrome cannot be used.
  - **En-route alternate**: to land after an **abnormal or emergency condition en route**.
  - **Destination alternate**: if it becomes impossible or inadvisable to land at the **intended destination**.
  - The **departure aerodrome may also be an en-route or destination alternate** for that flight.
- **ATS reporting office**: unit that **receives reports on ATS and flight plans** submitted before departure.

### When a flight plan is required
- Information on an intended flight shall be given to ATS in the form of a **flight plan**. Submit one before:
  - any flight (or part) **provided with air traffic control service**;
  - any **IFR flight within advisory airspace**;
  - any flight in designated areas/routes when ATS requires it for **flight information, alerting and SAR**;
  - any flight in designated areas/routes when required for **co-ordination with military units or ATS of adjacent States** (to avoid **interception** for identification);
  - any flight **across international borders**.
- Before departure the **operator** ensures the aircraft has the right approval where **RNP / RVSM / RCP / RSP** specifications apply (**RNP approval**, **RVSM approval**, **RCP approval**) and all conditions are met.
- For ATC or advisory service: submit **at least 60 minutes before departure**.

### Adherence, changes and deviations
- Adhere to the **current flight plan** unless a change is requested and **cleared by ATC**, or an **emergency** needs immediate action (then notify ATS as soon as possible that **emergency authority** was used).
- **Changes**: all changes to an **IFR** flight plan or a **controlled VFR** flight must be reported as soon as practicable; for other VFR flights, **significant changes**. Incorrect **fuel endurance or persons on board** at departure is a significant change.
- An **ATC clearance** is obtained before a controlled flight, requested by **submitting a flight plan**.
- **Deviations**:
  - **Off track** → adjust heading **forthwith** to regain track.
  - Deviation from **ATC-assigned Mach/IAS** → inform ATS **immediately**.
  - Sustained **Mach/TAS** differs by **± M0.02** or **± 19 km/h (10 kt) TAS** or more from the plan → inform ATS.
  - **Time estimate** for the next reporting point, FIR boundary or destination (whichever first) changes by **more than 2 minutes** → notify ATS as soon as possible (unless **ADS-C** is active: then the ATSU is informed automatically by data link beyond the **ADS event contract** thresholds).
- **Change requests** contain: **level change** — identification, new level and Mach/TAS at it, revised estimates; **Mach/TAS change** — identification, requested Mach/TAS; **route change, destination unchanged** — identification, flight rules, new route from the point where it starts, revised estimates, other information; **destination changed** — the same plus the new destination and **alternate aerodrome(s)**.

### Weather below VMC (controlled VFR flight)
- (a) Request an **amended clearance** to continue in VMC to destination/alternate or to leave controlled airspace; or
- (b) if none can be obtained, **continue in VMC** and tell ATC you are leaving the airspace or landing at the **nearest suitable aerodrome**; or
- (c) in a **control zone**, request **special VFR**; or
- (d) request clearance to fly **IFR**.

### Changing flight rules
- **VFR → IFR**: if a flight plan was filed, pass the changes to the current plan; or, when required, **submit a flight plan and get a clearance** before proceeding IFR in controlled airspace.
- **IFR → VFR**: notify ATS specifically that the IFR flight is cancelled. Accepted only on a pilot message containing **"CANCELLING MY IFR FLIGHT"** plus any changes. ATS must **never invite** a change to VFR, directly or by inference.
- ATS replies only **"IFR FLIGHT CANCELLED AT (time)"**. If VMC may not be maintained, ATS says **"INSTRUMENT METEOROLOGICAL CONDITIONS REPORTED (or forecast) IN THE VICINITY OF (location)"**.
- The receiving ATS unit tells all other ATS units the IFR plan was addressed to (except those already passed).

### Closing a flight plan
- Make an **arrival report**, in person or by radio, **as early as possible after landing**, to the ATS unit at the arrival aerodrome, for any flight whose plan covers the whole flight or the remaining portion to destination.

### India: filing flight plans
- ATS must be **informed before any flight**; use the **ICAO model flight plan**.
- Submit **at least 60 minutes before departure (EOBT)**; before departure to an **ATS reporting office**, or in flight on the air-ground frequency, unless **repetitive/stored** plans are arranged.
- **Scheduled flights**: printed/electronic plans accepted; the **operator** obtains **ATS, COMM and MET briefing** and keeps the documents as records for **6 months**. ATC may still require the PIC/crew to **report in person** for special briefing.
- **Non-scheduled flights**: signed and submitted by the **PIC**, or by an **approved flight dispatcher** of the operator/authorised handling agency. Those persons may take ATS, COMM and MET briefing.
- **Local/training/test flights** by operators: filed as for **non-scheduled** flights.
- **Flying-club training flights within the aerodrome traffic zone**: may be filed **by telephone** with aircraft identification, flight rules, names of pilots and trainees, persons on board, **duration** and **endurance**.
- Where no ATZ or local flying area exists, the **vicinity of the aerodrome** for VFR = **5 NM radius** of the ARP, up to **3000 ft AGL**.
- Club/GA **cross-country** flights from fields without an ATS reporting office: file with the **FIC by phone**.
- Include **accumulated elapsed times to FIR boundary points in Item 18**.
- **Non-scheduled flights into/across Indian airspace**: state the **DGCA authority (YA No.)** or **AHQ authority (AOR No.)** in **Item 18**.

### Repetitive flight plans (RPLs)
- Only for **IFR** flights operated regularly on the **same day(s) of consecutive weeks on at least 10 occasions**, or **every day for at least 10 consecutive days**, with highly stable elements.
- Cover the **entire flight** from departure to destination; used only when **all ATS authorities concerned agree**. For international flights, adjacent States must already use (or start using) RPLs; arrangements by **bilateral, multilateral or regional agreement**.
- A flight plan form based on the ICAO model is used (a different form may be used for RPL listings); printed, with **English text** in addition to the State language.

### Submission
- **Not more than 120 hours before EOBT**. Before departure: to the **ATS reporting office** at the departure aerodrome (or the unit serving it).
- **Delay**: **30 minutes** beyond EOBT (**controlled** flight) or **1 hour** (**uncontrolled**) → **amend** the plan or file a new one and cancel the old.
- **In flight**: to the ATS unit in charge of the **FIR / control area / advisory area or route** (or its aeronautical telecommunication station); otherwise via another unit for retransmission. The ATS authority may limit in-flight filing in busy airspace.
- Filed for **ATC service** → **wait for a clearance**; filed for **advisory service** → wait for **acknowledgment of receipt**.
- **Acceptance**: the first ATS unit **checks format and data conventions, completeness and accuracy**, makes it acceptable if needed and **indicates acceptance** to the originator.
- **Intermediate stops**: initial clearance limit = **first destination**; new clearance for each stage. Later stages become active for ATS/SAR only when **departure from the previous aerodrome is notified** (scheduled flights through several control areas may be cleared through intermediate stops after **ACC co-ordination**).

### Completing the form: general rules
- Start in the **first space**; leave unused spaces blank. **All clock times: 4 figures UTC. Elapsed times: 4 figures (hh mm).**
- The **shaded area before Item 3** is for ATS/COM.
- "Aerodrome" includes other sites (helicopters, balloons).
- Complete **Items 7 to 18**; **Item 19** when required. Item numbers are **not consecutive** (they match **field types in ATS messages**).

- **ICAO operator designator + flight number** (e.g. **KLM511, NGA213, JTR25**) when the RT call sign is the telephony designator + flight number (KLM511, NIGERIA 213, JESTER 25); **or**
- **Nationality/common mark + registration** (e.g. **EIAKO, 4XBCD, N2567GA**) when the RT call sign is that alone (e.g. CGAJS) or preceded by the operator's telephony designator (BLIZZARD CGAJS), or the aircraft has **no radio**.

Plus letters, e.g. **A** GBAS landing, **B** LPV (APV with SBAS), **C** LORAN C, **D** DME, **E1–E3** ACARS (FMC WPR, D-FIS, PDC), **F** ADF, **G** GNSS (augmentation in Item 18 **NAV/**), **H** HF RTF, **I** inertial, **J1–J7** CPDLC (ATN / FANS 1/A with VDL, HFDL, SATCOM), **K** MLS, **L** ILS, **M1–M3** ATC RTF SATCOM (INMARSAT / MTSAT / Iridium), **O** VOR, **P1–P9** reserved for RCP, **R** PBN approved (levels in **PBN/**), **T** TACAN, **U** UHF RTF, **V** VHF RTF, **W** RVSM approved, **X** MNPS approved, **Y** VHF with 8.33 kHz spacing, **Z** other (details after **COM/, NAV/, DAT/**).

## 17. Air Data Systems (Pressure Instruments)
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 1.*

### The pitot-static system
- Aircraft constantly meet **pressure changes** as they climb, descend, accelerate or decelerate. The **pitot-static system** (sensitive to airspeed, altitude and rate of altitude change) supplies the pressures shown on the cockpit instruments.
- Two major parts: the **impact pressure chamber and lines (pitot tube)** and the **static pressure chamber and lines**. Together they feed the **altimeter**, the **vertical speed indicator (VSI / VVI)** and the **airspeed indicator (ASI)**.
- **Pitot tube**: senses **impact (ram) air pressure**; mounted where there is **minimum disturbance**, usually **near the wing leading edge**, piped to the **back of the ASI case**. Susceptible to **dirt, water and ice** — aircraft for instrument flight must have a **heated pitot tube**; inspect the tube and **test the heater before flight**. Its opening is **nearly perpendicular** to the relative wind.
- **Static port**: usually **flush on the side of the fuselage**, sensing the pressure of the **still air**; vents on both sides compensate for erratic static changes with **attitude**. Its opening is always **parallel to the relative airflow**.
- **Alternate static source**: inside the cockpit (**unpressurised aircraft only**) or outside; selected by the pilot (switch) if the main source is blocked by ice etc. Cabin pressure is **lower** than outside (airflow over the cabin), so on the alternate source:
  - **Altimeter** reads **higher** than actual;
  - **VSI** momentarily shows a **climb**, then settles back;
  - **ASI** reads **greater** than normal.

### Air data system
- Used on more sophisticated aircraft; built on the pitot-static system. **Measures, processes and converts** aerodynamic and thermodynamic sensations into **electrical signals, synchro outputs or digital codes**, to drive flight instruments and many other systems.
- May include: **separate systems for pilot and co-pilot** displays with **comparison monitoring**; **self-test** circuits; **internal failure-monitoring** circuits.
- **Components**:
  - **Sensors** — measure the surrounding atmosphere: all systems use a **pitot tube, static ports and a temperature probe**; complex ones add an **angle-of-attack sensor**.
  - **Transducers** — convert pressure, temperature and angles to **voltages, synchro outputs or digital pulses**; their accuracy governs the whole system.
  - **Computer** — calculates **TAS, Mach number, corrected static pressure and corrected OAT**; originates correction signals to transducers; drives displays; feeds **navigation computers**; controls **pressurisation**; gives inputs to the **AFCS** and **engine fuel control units**.
- **Outputs**: **pressure altitude** (static pressure corrected to the ICAO Standard Atmosphere); **airspeed** (IAS, or TAS for DR/inertial navigation); **air density** (gas laws, for engine controls); **Mach number** (from pitot and static pressures); **air temperature** (corrected for **frictional heating and compression** at the probe); **angle of attack** (measured AoA corrected for airspeed = true AoA); **rates of change of altitude and speed**.
- ADC inputs/outputs diagram (book): inputs — pitot pressure, static pressure, total air temperature, power supply, aircraft type data (VMO), BITE isolation switch; outputs — displays, flight control systems, automatic reporting, GPWS, autothrottle, yaw damper, cabin pressurisation, flight data recorder, control and display unit with BITE.

### CADC errors (common to all instruments)
- **Position error**: varies with aircraft **type and external configuration**; flight tests plot it against airspeed, altitude and configuration, and the manufacturer builds a **correcting circuit** for the static signal → **calibrated airspeed, actual TAS, calibrated altitude, true Mach**. So a CADC is **tailored to one aircraft type** and not usable in others.
- **Scale (instrument) error**: tied to a particular set of **altitude aneroids**, varies with altitude; plotted for each CADC and reduced with a **corrective camshaft** on the altitude output.

### Errors specific to the ASI and VSI
- **ASI density error**: flying from **high to low density**, the ASI **under-reads** (dynamic pressure = ½ρV²). For the **same IAS, TAS increases** as density falls, and vice versa.
- **ASI compressibility error**: air brought to rest in the pitot tube is **compressed**, raising pitot pressure → ASI **over-reads** (IAS too high). **Negligible at or below 300 kt TAS** and ignored.
- **VSI transonic jump**: near **Mach 1** a shock wave makes the static pressure drop as it passes the static source → a **brief false jump** on the VSI (a positive sign the aircraft is close to the speed of sound).

### Exam traps
- **Pitot** feeds the **ASI only**; **static** feeds **ASI, altimeter and VSI**.
- Alternate static inside the cabin: **all three over-read** (VSI a brief climb).
- Compressibility → ASI **over-reads**; ignore below **300 kt TAS**.

## 18. Altimeter
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 2.*

### Purpose and principle
- Gives the aircraft's **height with respect to a set datum**. It is essentially a **barometer**: measures **static pressure** and displays it as altitude.
- At high cruising altitudes pressure changes are **very small**, so amplification is needed → the **servo altimeter** (electrical amplifying circuit).
- The **sub-scale setting** corrects for local pressure variations: about **30 ft per hPa** the sub-scale differs from the actual pressure.

### Simple (service) altimeter
- An **aneroid barometer** (evacuated capsule; air moves out, wafers contract/expand) in a **case connected to the static source**; needles (100 ft, 1000 ft pointers) driven through **linkages**; a **barometric scale (setting window)** and adjusting knob.
- Capsule holds a **vacuum (very low pressure)**; the **case** holds **static pressure**.
- Drawbacks: becomes **insensitive at higher altitude** (pressure falls more slowly with height) and suffers **lag error**.

### Sensitive altimeter
- Same principle, but sensitivity improved by **more than one capsule in series** and by a **vibrator / rocking / knocking device** (reduces friction in the linkages).
- Drawbacks of simple and sensitive altimeters (**lag**): **inertia of the capsule (hysteresis)** and **mechanical linkage** friction.

### Servo altimeter
- Lag error of service and sensitive types (linkages, capsule inertia) is **virtually eliminated** by an **induction pick-off / electromechanical pick-off** device (E- and I-bar transducer → electrical signal → follow-up system driving a counter and pointer).
- **Tolerance at sea level ± 60 ft.** The **transducer removes lag error**. More accurate than a conventional altimeter.
- Pressure experienced over hilly terrain is called **hydrostatic pressure**.

### Errors — "BLIP PBT"
- An altimeter showing a **50 ft error** when set to the current setting is **unserviceable**. In flight, set the nearest source (tower, METAR etc.).
- **B — Blockage**: indicates a **constant altitude** in climb or descent; the pointer **freezes** (static blocked at 9000 ft, aircraft at 10 000 ft → reads **9000 ft**).
- **L — Lag**: from **hysteresis and mechanical friction**; virtually removed in the servo altimeter by the **electromechanical pick-off**.
- **I — Instrument**: inherent/manufacturing error.
- **P — Position**: dynamic-pressure interference at the static source, from (a) **incorrect static source position**, (b) **induced** causes (slip/skid, low speed, high or low angle of attack, standby/alternate source), (c) **ground effect** (wing-tip vortices and airflow around the aircraft). Position error makes the altimeter **always under-read**.
- **P — Pressure**: **high → low pressure**: reads **higher** than actual; **low → high**: reads **lower** than actual.
- **B — Barometric**: sub-scale differs from the actual pressure at the point → wrong indication.
- **T — Temperature**: **high → low temperature**: altimeter **over-reads** (H-L-H). Calibrated to **ISA temperature and pressure lapse rate**; **no altimeter setting compensates** for temperature deviation. At constant indicated altitude from warm to cold air the aircraft **descends** and is truly **lower** than indicated. "**Hot to cold, don't be bold**" / "**cold kills**". The opposite from cold to hot.

### From the questions
- A 1 hPa accuracy ≈ **± 27 ft at MSL**, **± 50 ft at 10 000 ft**.
- **White zebra (cross-hatch) flag** appears **below 10 000 ft**.
- **Bimetallic strip** compensates for **thermal expansion of the mechanical linkages**.
- Unpressurised aircraft, static blocked: altimeter **freezes**; remedy: **alternate static**, last resort **break the VSI glass** (never the altimeter's).
- Two static vents, one blocked: **over-reads when side-slipping towards the blocked source** (the clear port is then on the lee side).
- **Pressure altitude**: altitude in the **standard atmosphere** corresponding to the pressure at that point. Constant flight level = **constant outside pressure**.
- Pitot blockage does **not** affect the altimeter.

### Exam traps
- **High to low (pressure or temperature): look out below** — the altimeter over-reads.
- Servo: **induction pick-off**, **± 60 ft** at sea level, lag removed.
- Vibrator: reduces **friction / instrument error**, not hysteresis.

## 19. Air Speed Indicator (ASI) and Machmeter
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 3.*

### Principle
- **Purpose**: to give **indicated airspeed (IAS)**. **Principle**: measures the **difference between pitot and static pressure** (the dynamic pressure).
- **Pitot pressure (P) = dynamic (D) + static (S)**; the dynamic part is also called **pitot excess (PE)**, so P = PE + S.
- The **pitot tube**, an open-ended tube **parallel to the longitudinal axis**, faces into the airstream and feeds pitot pressure to the airspeed **capsule**; the **case** is fed with **static** pressure. The capsule therefore distorts by an amount equal to the **dynamic pressure**; the distortion is amplified and shown as IAS. The ASI is a **differential pressure gauge**.
- **Dynamic pressure = ½ρV²** (V = TAS, ρ = air density).
- **Square-law compensation**: the pressure driving the mechanism is **proportional to the square of the airspeed**, so the scale/mechanism compensates to show small dynamic pressures (evenly readable scale).
- The ASI **under-reads about 2% per 1000 ft** of altitude (density falls).

### From IAS to groundspeed
- **ASIR + instrument error → IAS**; **IAS + position/pressure error → RAS / CAS** (rectified / calibrated airspeed); **CAS + compressibility → EAS**; **EAS + density → TAS**; **TAS + wind → groundspeed**.
- **Calibrated airspeed (CAS)**: IAS corrected for **instrument and installation (position) error** — largest at **high angles of attack**; correction charts in the POH.
- **True airspeed (TAS)**: CAS corrected for **density (altitude and temperature)**; used for flight planning.

### ASI markings
- **White arc**: flap operating range — lower limit **VS0** (power-off stall, flaps and gear down), upper limit **VFE** (max flap-extended speed).
- **Green arc**: normal operating range — **VS1** (power-off stall, flaps and gear up) to **VNO** (max structural cruising speed).
- **Yellow arc**: caution range, smooth air only — **VNO to VNE**.
- **Red line**: **VNE**, never exceed.
- **Multi-engine**: a **red radial** near the bottom of the green arc — **VMC** (minimum control speed with the critical engine inoperative); a **blue radial** — **VYSE** (best rate of climb, critical engine inoperative).
- **Manoeuvring speed (VA)** and **VLO** are **not marked**.
- Jets: a **barber pole** (moving red-and-white hatched pointer) shows the applicable limit (**VMO** versus altitude): it moves **down as you climb** and **up as you descend**.

### Errors — "BLIP DC"
- **B — Blockage**:
  - **Static blocked**: climb → **under-reads**; descent → **over-reads**; level → correct.
  - **Pitot blocked, drain clear**: airspeed **gradually drops to zero**.
  - **Pitot and drain blocked, static clear**: level → **freezes**; climb → **over-reads**; descent → **under-reads** — the ASI **acts like an altimeter**.
  - Pitot, drain and static all blocked: **freezes**.
  - **SOD SUC PUD POC**: Static — Over-reads in Descent; Static — Under-reads in Climb; Pitot — Under-reads in Descent; Pitot — Over-reads in Climb.
- **L — Lag**; **I — Instrument** (manufacturing).
- **P — Position/pressure error**: dynamic pressure interfering at the static source; makes the ASI **under-read**.
- **D — Density error**: from high to low density the ASI **under-reads**; for the **same IAS, TAS increases** as density falls, and vice versa.
- **C — Compressibility error**: at **TAS ≥ 300 kt** the air brought to rest in the pitot tube is compressed, raising pitot pressure → ASI **over-reads**; the correction is always **negative** (subtracted) and gives **EAS**.

### Machmeter
- Shows **Mach number = TAS ÷ local speed of sound (LSS)**. **Principle**: the **ratio of dynamic to static pressure, (P − S) ÷ S**.
- Two capsules: an **airspeed capsule** (dynamic pressure) and an **altitude capsule** (static); a **ratio (ranging) arm** divides one by the other. It is an **ASI with an altitude capsule**.
- **Errors**: **blockage, instrument, position** only — **no density or temperature error**. Blockages follow **SOD SUC PUD POC** as for the ASI.
- The speed of sound depends **only on temperature**.
- **Regimes**: subsonic < M 0.75; transonic M 0.75–1.2; sonic M 1.0; supersonic M 1.2–5; hypersonic M 5–10; high-hypersonic > M 10.
- **MMO**: maximum operating Mach number, kept below M 1 because shock waves form well before M 1. **Critical Mach number (MCRIT)**: the Mach number at which the airflow over some part of the wing **first reaches M 1** — a shock wave first appears.
- **VMO limits low down, MMO high up**: a jet descending at maximum speed is limited **first by MMO, then by VMO** below a certain level. Under standard conditions, TAS 562 kt = **M 0.85 at sea level** and **M 1.0 at 40 000 ft**.
- Jets need both an ASI and a Machmeter with red lines, or a combined instrument with concentric scales, or a barber pole.

### From the questions
- Pitot blocked by ice in a climb: ASI and Machmeter **over-read / increase** regardless of actual speed; in a descent they **decrease**.
- Static blocked in a climb: ASI under-reads, Machmeter **under-indicates**.
- At **MSL in ISA, CAS = TAS**. As an aircraft climbs, **TAS increases** for a given IAS.
- **VMO is expressed in CAS**. ADC advantages over a plain pitot-static system: **position and compressibility correction, reduced lag, supplying many instruments**.
- Turbulent flow around a pressure head causes about **95% of position error**.
- Compressibility error is corrected using a **navigation computer**.
- Descending at constant CAS, **dynamic pressure stays constant**.

### Exam traps
- ASI: **pitot in the capsule, static in the case**; measures **P − S**.
- **SOD SUC PUD POC**; pitot + drain blocked → behaves **like an altimeter**.
- White **VS0–VFE**, green **VS1–VNO**, yellow **VNO–VNE**, red **VNE**, blue **VYSE**.
- Compressibility → **over-reads** above **300 kt TAS**; correction negative → EAS.
- Machmeter = **(P − S) ÷ S**; **no temperature or density error**; LSS depends on **temperature only**.
- High level limited by **MMO**, low level by **VMO**.

## 20. Vertical Speed Indicator (VSI)
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 4.*

### Principle
- A **rate-of-pressure-change** instrument: shows any departure from a constant pressure level as a **rate of climb or descent in feet per minute**; gives **trend and rate** information.
- A **static pressure differential** instrument: the **capsule** gets **direct static** pressure; the **case** gets static through a **calibrated leak** (metered orifice, choke), so case pressure changes **more slowly** than capsule pressure.
- **Climb**: static falls; capsule pressure falls at once while the case lags (higher) → the **capsule contracts** → needle **up**. **Descent**: capsule pressure rises first → the **capsule expands** → needle **down**. **Level**: case and capsule pressures equalise → needle returns to **zero**.
- It measures the **difference between the pressure inside and outside a capsule**. Pitot pressure plays no part.
- **Pre-flight**: needle horizontal at zero; serviceable within **± 200 fpm** between −20 °C and +50 °C. A small ground error can be set with the **adjusting screw** on the front and flown with as a new reference. In flight, cross-check with height gained over a timed period.

### Errors — "BLIP T"
- **B — Blockage**: static blocked → after a short time the pressures equalise and the VSI reads **zero** in climb or descent.
- **L — Lag**: the **predominant error** — the instrument works on the principle of lag. Readings are wrong for some seconds after starting or ending a climb or descent (**time lag plus manoeuvre-induced error**).
- **I — Instrument**: built-in error.
- **P — Position**: dynamic pressure at the static source.
- **T — Transonic jump**: near **Mach 1** a shock wave passing the static source drops static pressure briefly → a **brief false jump** — a positive sign the aircraft is close to the speed of sound.

### Instantaneous VSI (IVSI)
- **Dashpot accelerometers** (a piston responding at once to pitch changes) give an **instant change of capsule pressure** → **lag is removed**; more reliable when the aircraft **starts** a climb or descent.
- **Disadvantages**: the dashpots also sense **turn acceleration** — in large bank (**over 40°**) it first shows a **climb**; held in the turn it settles to zero, then shows a **descent** on rolling out. It **oscillates in turbulence**.

### Leaks and blockages (from the questions)
- **Metered orifice partly blocked**: case pressure lags more → VSI **over-reads in climb and descent**.
- **Case leak in a pressurised aircraft**: cabin pressure enters the case → reads **too high in a climb, too low in a descent**, and shows a climb even in level flight.
- **Case leak in an unpressurised aircraft**: case equalises with cabin (≈ static) → reads **zero**.
- **Static pipe partly blocked**: VSI **too low** when climbing or descending. **Pitot blockage**: **no effect**.
- Static vent blocked in an unpressurised aircraft: use the **standby (alternate) static source**; last resort, break the **VSI glass**.
- Rule of thumb: **ROD ≈ TAS (kt) × 101 × tan(slope)** → 100 kt on a 3° slope ≈ **500 fpm**.

### Exam traps
- Capsule **direct static**, case **static through a calibrated leak**; climb → capsule **contracts**.
- Static blocked → **zero**. Lag is the main error; **IVSI** removes it with **dashpots** but shows a **climb in a steep turn**.
- Pressurised-aircraft case leak → **high in climb, low in descent**; unpressurised → **zero**.
- Transonic jump = brief **false** reading near Mach 1.

## 21. Gyroscope and Gyro Instruments
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 5.*

### Gyroscopic principles
- A **gyroscope** is a wheel with its weight concentrated at the **periphery**, spun at high speed with low-friction bearings; mounted in **gimbals**. A universally (freely) mounted gyro has **three planes of freedom**; a restricted (semi-rigid) one has one plane held fixed to the base.
- **Rigidity in space**: the spin axis keeps its direction in space (Newton's first law). Rigidity increases with **rotor speed (RPM)**, **mass** and **radius** (moment of inertia), i.e. mass concentrated at the rim. **Attitude and heading** instruments use rigidity; their rotors are universally mounted.
- **Precession**: a force on the rim acts **90° ahead in the direction of rotation**. The rate of precession is **proportional to the applied force** and **inversely proportional to rotor speed**. **Rate instruments** (turn indicator, turn coordinator) use precession.
- A gyro gives **no useful indication about its own spin axis**; the rotor is kept **at right angles** to the axis to be measured. The number of degrees of freedom equals the **number of gimbals**.
- **Power**: **vacuum, pressure or electrical**. Vacuum/pressure systems spin the rotor with an air jet on its vanes, usually **4.5–5.5 in Hg**; an **engine-driven vane pump**, regulator, filter and gauge. A dirty filter → **lower** gauge reading.

### Types of gyro
- **Free (space) gyro**: freedom in all three planes, axis fixed relative to space — **INS/IRS**.
- **Tied gyro**: axis held by an **external force** — **DGI**.
- **Earth gyro**: axis held by **gravity** — **artificial horizon**.
- **Rate gyro**: freedom in **two planes**, indicates in the third (**one degree of freedom**, one gimbal) — **turn and slip indicator / turn coordinator**.

| Instrument | Gyro | Spin axis | RPM (book) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Artificial horizon | earth gyro | **vertical** | electric **22 000–22 500**, air **15 000** |
| DGI | tied gyro | **horizontal** | **12 000** |
| Turn and slip | rate gyro | **horizontal** | usually electric |

### Artificial horizon (attitude indicator)
- Uses **rigidity** for indication and **precession** for control (erection). **Inner gimbal** — lateral freedom (pitch); **outer gimbal** — longitudinal freedom (bank); the **sky plate** is on the outer gimbal; the **miniature aircraft** is fixed to the case glass.
- **Limits**: old type pitch **± 55°**, roll **± 90°**; modern pitch **± 85°**, roll **complete (360°)**. **Caging knob**: mechanically locks the gimbals to protect the gyro and allows quick re-erection (older types).
- **Errors (air-driven gyro rotating anticlockwise seen from above)**:
  - **Acceleration** (left/right pendulous vanes): shows **climb and bank to the right**; deceleration → **descent and bank to the left**. Also shown during the **take-off roll**.
  - **Turning error** (fore/aft pendulous vanes): shows a **climb** in left and right turns; size depends on speed, rate of turn and erection rate.
  - Through a turn at constant bank: **90°** → climb and **under-bank**; **180°** → climb (maximum pitch error), bank nearly correct with slight bank the opposite way; **270°** → climb and **over-bank**; **360°** → correct.
  - Rolling out (visually) after a **180° steep turn to the right**: shows a **slight climb and bank/turn to the left** (3–5°), corrected quickly by erection.

### Directional gyro indicator (DGI)
- **Horizontal-axis tied gyro**, **two gimbals, two degrees of freedom**; rigidity for indication, precession for control. **No north-seeking element**: align with the magnetic compass after start and at least **every 15 minutes** (adjustment knob). Slaved versions are kept aligned by a **flux gate**.
- **Wander** = shift of the gyro axis: **drift** (horizontal plane) and **topple** (vertical plane). **Real wander**: imperfections, friction, worn bearings, uneven rotor mass. **Apparent wander**: **earth rotation (earth-rate)** and **movement over the earth (transport)**.
- **Earth-rate drift = 15° × sin latitude per hour** (maximum 15°/h at the poles).
- **Transport drift = groundspeed ÷ 60 × tan latitude** (°/h); depends on groundspeed, track and latitude.
- **Latitude nut**: a weight on the inner gimbal that compensates **earth-rate drift** for one chosen latitude (set in the workshop): **north — in, south — out (SONI)**. Total drift = real + latitude-nut correction + earth-rate + transport.
- **Gimbal error**: from **bank or pitch** attitude.

### Turn and slip indicator and turn coordinator
- **TSI**: rate of turn in degrees per second; **horizontal-axis rate gyro**, **one degree of freedom**, usually **electric**; uses **precession** against a **calibrated spring**. Shows the correct turn direction even on the ground.
- **Turn coordinator**: gimbal inclined about **30°** so it senses **roll and yaw** — shows **bank while rolling in or out**, and rate once established.
- **Rate 1 = 3°/s** (2-minute turn); rate 2 = 6°/s; rate 3 = 9°/s; rate 4 = 12°/s. Rate 1 bank **≈ TAS ÷ 10 + 7** (not more than 25°).
- For the same rate, bank **increases with TAS**; rate of turn at constant bank **depends on TAS only** (not weight). Turn needle reading = yaw rate in a coordinated turn.
- **Errors — "ROGOTU"**: **R**PM high → **O**ver-reads; **G** (load factor) high → **O**ver-reads; spring **T**ension high → **U**nder-reads.
- **Slip ball (inclinometer)**: a ball in a curved liquid-filled tube; shows the balance of gravity and centrifugal force — **not** bank. **Slip**: ball **inside** the turn (too much bank / too little rudder / rate too slow). **Skid**: ball **outside** (too much rudder, rate too fast). "**Step on the ball**". Taxiing: needle shows the turn, ball goes the **opposite** way.

### Exam traps
- Rigidity ↑ with **RPM, mass, radius**; precession rate ∝ **force ÷ RPM**, 90° on in the direction of rotation.
- **AH = vertical earth gyro**; **DGI = horizontal tied gyro, 2 gimbals**; **TSI = horizontal rate gyro, 1 degree of freedom**.
- Air-driven AH: **acceleration → climb + right bank**; turns → **climb**; 90° under-bank, 270° over-bank, 360° correct.
- DGI drift **15 sin lat °/h**; latitude nut **SONI**; check against the compass **every 15 min**.
- Rate 1 = **3°/s**, bank ≈ **TAS/10 + 7**. **ROGOTU**. Ball **in = slip**, **out = skid**.

## 22. Inertial Navigation (INS / IRS)
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 6.*

### Principle
- A form of **dead reckoning** using **accelerometers** (acceleration) and **gyroscopes** along **three perpendicular axes**. Needs **no external reference**, nothing magnetic, no plotting, unaffected by weather; corrections for movement over the earth and the earth's own rotation are built in.
- **Acceleration integrated with time → velocity; velocity integrated with time → distance**, from the known start position → latitude and longitude.
- Gimballed INS: accelerometers kept **level on a stable platform** by **three rate gyros**, gimbals and torquers, so only motion **tangential to the earth** is measured. The computer corrects for **earth rotation, the spherical (and non-spherical) earth, Coriolis, centrifugal and centripetal** accelerations. It stores **magnetic variation** for all locations and is a **great-circle navigator**.
- **Inputs**: start position (lat/long) and waypoints; GPS position; **barometric altitude** (stabilises vertical velocity and inertial altitude); **TAS** from the ADC (for **wind**).
- **Outputs**: pitch, roll, yaw; true and magnetic heading; TAS and GS; lat, long and inertial altitude; **wind velocity and drift angle**; vertical speed. Used by the FMS, autopilot, autothrottle, ADI/HSI, EFIS, weather radar (stabilisation), anti-skid/autobrake, FDR and others.

### Modes and alignment
- **Gyro compassing / pre-departure**: aligning the platform with the **local meridian**; the aircraft must be **stationary and level**.
- **OFF → STBY** (warm-up; present position entered to **6 seconds of arc**) → **ALIGN** (levelling and alignment; **must not be moved**, though gusts, loading and refuelling don't matter) → **NAV** (READY NAV light; navigation computed and shown on the CDU).
- **ATT mode**: no position or groundspeed; still gives **acceleration, angular rate and attitude**. Heading must be entered manually on the CDU and reset about **every 10 minutes**. Used after a critical fault, or if the system is switched off or misaligned in flight.
- **BATT light**: power failure; a self-contained **15-minute** battery back-up.
- Entering the local coordinates for a strap-down unit positions the **computing trihedron** relative to the earth.

### Errors and Schuler
- **Schuler loop**: an earth-horizontal platform behaves like a pendulum as long as the earth's radius — period **84.4 minutes**. Schuler errors are **bounded** (they oscillate, not grow).
- Older platform INS: **1–2 nm/h**; modern strap-down RLG: about **0.5 nm/h** (over 5 h, ~2.5 nm is still good; GPS does far better).
- Other errors: initial levelling and alignment, input inaccuracies, real wander, drift, transport wander, Coriolis, earth rotation, integration, accelerometer, latitude and height.
- **Radial error rate = distance between the ramp position and the INS position ÷ hours in NAV mode.**

### IRS with ring laser gyros (strap-down)
- **No stabilised platform**: three accelerometers and three **ring laser gyros** are bolted to the airframe, aligned with the **pitch, roll and yaw** axes; the platform is stabilised **mathematically**, not mechanically.
- **Advantages**: solid-state, no moving parts, very reliable and light; senses **0.004–400°/s**; **no mechanical precession, cannot topple**; low power; **faster alignment** (no spin-up, operational within a second); insensitive to **g**; longer life; integrates with GPS; easy unit replacement.
- **Disadvantages**: **laser lock** at low rotation rates — cured by a **dither motor**; more expensive.
- **IRU**: the computer integrating IRS outputs for the FMS and flight controls.

### Advantages and disadvantages of inertial systems
- **For**: instantaneous, continuous position and velocity; totally self-contained; all latitudes and weathers; independent of manoeuvres; W/V shown when TAS is fed; follows the **great circle**.
- **Against**: built-in drift; **data can be inserted on the ground only** — shut down or misaligned in flight it gives **attitude only**; many error sources; costly; alignment simple when stationary but **difficult above 75° latitude** and in flight.

### Exam traps
- Integrate **once → velocity, twice → distance**. Sensors measure **acceleration**.
- Align **stationary**; NAV before moving; lost in flight → **ATT mode** (attitude, rates, acceleration only).
- TAS input is only for **wind**. Schuler **84.4 min**, bounded.
- RLG: **dither motor** against **laser lock**; no precession or topple; fast alignment.
- Error rate = **radial error ÷ hours in NAV**.

## 23. Altitude Alert and Warning Systems (Radio Altimeter, TCAS, GPWS)
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 7.*

### Radio (radar) altimeter — RADALT
- An **absolute altimeter** (terrain-clearance indicator): measures **height above the ground or water directly below**, however uneven; **independent of barometric settings**. Self-contained, aircraft-based.
- Also feeds the **automatic flight system** for **autoland** (with ILS/MLS) and supplies height and rate of change of height to the **GPWS** — it is **crucial** to GPWS.
- **FMCW** (frequency-modulated continuous wave) in the **SHF band, 4200–4400 MHz** (average **4300 MHz**), **centimetric** wavelength. The carrier is swept **± 50 MHz, 300 times a second**; a wide sweep gives a measurable frequency difference at very low height.
- **Principle**: by the time the echo returns, the transmitter frequency has changed; the **frequency difference** between transmitted and received signals is proportional to the **time delay** and so to **height** (no Doppler — there is no relative motion).
- Being continuous wave it needs **separate transmit and receive aerials** (flush horn aerials), far enough apart to avoid interference; about **1 watt**. The conical/elliptical beam reaches vertically below even at **± 30° pitch, ± 60° roll**; height comes from the shortest (vertical) path.
- **Range 0–2500 ft**: above that, signals from different modulation cycles **overlap** (the receiver gets the same frequency it is transmitting). Steps of 5 ft. **Accuracy ± 2 ft from 0 to 500 ft**, ± 3% + 5 ft above 500 ft.
- **Indicator**: logarithmic scale, expanded 0–500 ft. The pointer goes **behind a mask** above 2500 ft, on a transmitter fault, or when switched off. **Failure flag** for too much noise, airframe reflections or power loss (needle disappears, possibly with an audio warning). **Press-to-test** swings to a preset height (**ground only**). **Low-height (DH) warning**: light below the set height; an alert tone rising from about 100 ft above DH stops abruptly at DH.
- Calibrated to show the height of the **lowest (main) wheels in the landing configuration**; with the gear **retracted** it **under-reads** (shows less than the actual clearance). It gives clearance **below** the aircraft, **not terrain ahead**.

### TCAS / ACAS
- **Purpose**: aural and visual alerts of **proximate traffic**, independent of ATC. **1030/1090 MHz (UHF)**; the aircraft equipment is an **interrogator and a transponder**. Maximum range about **30 nm**.
- **TA (traffic advisory)**: no assessed threat yet. **RA (resolution advisory)**: a threat, with an action to take.
- **ACAS I**: **TAs only** ("TRAFFIC TRAFFIC"), range, approximate bearing, relative height (mode C/S); warns about **40 s** from a potential collision.
- **ACAS II**: TAs and **RAs in the vertical plane**; if both aircraft have **mode S**, the computers **coordinate** their RAs. Uses directional aerials, transponder, processor, CDU; power reduces in dense traffic.
- **ACAS III**: horizontal advisories too (mode S).
- **RA against a non-ACAS aircraft**: possible if it has an **altitude-reporting transponder (mode C or S)**.
- **Preventive RA** ("MONITOR VERTICAL SPEED", "maintain vertical speed") — prevents a conflict. **Corrective RA** ("CLIMB, CLIMB", "DESCEND, DESCEND", "INCREASE CLIMB", "LEVEL OFF", reversals "CLIMB NOW") — always shown on an **RA-VSI** or EFIS. Required rates: **1500 ft/min** (increase: **2500 ft/min**). "**CLEAR OF CONFLICT**" when it ends.
- Book notes: RA about **40 s** before collision; **no vertical RA below 1000 ft AGL**.
- **Symbols**: hollow cyan/white diamond — other traffic; **solid cyan diamond — proximate** (within 6 nm, ±1200 ft); **solid amber/yellow circle — TA**; **solid red square — RA**. The number is relative altitude in hundreds of feet (+ above, − below) and the arrow shows climbing or descending.

### GPWS
- **Purpose**: aural and visual alerts when the aircraft is getting **dangerously close to the ground**. Inputs: **radio altitude**, air data (VSI, airspeed/Mach), **ILS glideslope**, gear and flap position, DH setting. Active from **50 to 2450 (≈ 2500) ft** radio height.
- **Mode 1 — excessive descent rate**: "SINK RATE" (outer boundary), then "WHOOP WHOOP PULL UP" (inner).
- **Mode 2 — excessive terrain closure rate**: 2A flaps not in landing configuration, 2B flaps in landing configuration; "TERRAIN TERRAIN", then "PULL UP".
- **Mode 3 — altitude loss after take-off or go-around** (not in landing configuration, about 50–700 ft): "**DON'T SINK**".
- **Mode 4 — unsafe terrain clearance not in landing configuration**: 4A gear up → "TOO LOW, TERRAIN" / "TOO LOW, GEAR" (below about 500 ft); 4B gear down, flaps not landing → "TOO LOW, FLAPS" (below about 200 ft).
- **Mode 5 — descent below the ILS glideslope**: amber lights; soft "GLIDESLOPE" (below 1000 ft, more than **1.3 dots** low, half volume, can be cancelled), becoming loud and continuous (more than **2.5 dots**).
- **Mode 6 — descent below the selected minimum (DH)**: "MINIMUMS, MINIMUMS" (gear down); EGPWS adds "BANK ANGLE".
- **EGPWS (TAWS)**: all GPWS functions plus **terrain ahead** (position from the FMS/GPS and a terrain database), **bank angle** on approach (envelope shrinks below 150 ft), and **Mode 7 windshear** below 1500 ft. Mode 4C: alert if radio height falls below a proportion of its earlier value after take-off or go-around.
- **Caution (alert)**: stop or reduce the descent, analyse, act, inform. **Warning**: **maximum power**, climb at the pitch for **stick-shaker / pitch limit (maximum gradient)**, inform ATC.

### Exam traps
- RADALT: **FMCW 4200–4400 MHz (SHF, centimetric)**, two aerials, **frequency difference ∝ height**, no Doppler, 0–2500 ft, ±2 ft to 500 ft. Gear up → **under-reads**. Tested on the ground only.
- TCAS: TA = information; **RA = act**, vertical only in ACAS II; "Monitor/maintain vertical speed" = **preventive** RA. RA against any **mode C/S** transponder.
- GPWS modes: **1 sink rate, 2 terrain closure, 3 don't sink, 4 too low gear/flaps, 5 glideslope, 6 minimums/bank, 7 windshear**. Range **50–2450 ft**.

## 24. Power Plant and System Monitoring Instruments
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 8.*

### Purpose
- Engine instruments show the **health and performance** of the engine and keep it within **operating limits** (RPM, pressure, temperature must never be exceeded); logged data show trends for **preventive maintenance**. Multi-engine aircraft usually have a set of gauges per engine.

### Pressure
- Sensors: **diaphragm/capsule** (low pressures) or **Bourdon tube** (higher pressures). Reading is **direct** (pressure line to the cockpit) or **indirect** (sensor on the engine or firewall, electrical transmission).
- The two principal **direct-reading** pressure gauges: **Bourdon tube** and **capsule** types.
- **Oil pressure**: direct — a **Bourdon tube** filled with thin oil; indirect — a firewall diaphragm driving a **rheostat** and a **ratio meter** (two coils).
- **Bourdon tube**: a C-shaped or spiral tube of **oval cross-section**, one end fixed; pressure makes the section rounder and the tube **straighten**, moving the pointer.
- **Manifold pressure (MAP)**: needed with a controllable-pitch propeller to set power for the selected RPM; an aneroid capsule/bellows reading **absolute pressure in in Hg** (10–30 in; up to 50+ for turbocharged engines). It may show boost (above atmospheric) instead.
- **Fuel pressure**: like remote oil pressure; diaphragm sensor fitted **after the filter and pump**.
- **Colours**: MAP — **red radial = maximum take-off MAP**, **blue and green arcs = lean and rich mixture ranges**. Oil pressure — **red lines** at max and min, **green** normal. **Amber** = caution.

### Temperature
- Principles: **expansion of a solid** (bimetallic helix), **expansion of a liquid**, **change of electrical resistance** (platinum wire), **thermocouple EMF** (two dissimilar metals; EMF depends on the temperature **difference** between junctions), **vapour pressure** in a Bourdon tube, **radiation** (pyrometer, turbojets).
- Air temperature is mainly measured **electrically** (resistance or thermocouple). The probe is sited to avoid compressibility, but some **ram rise** remains; correcting the reading gives **COAT** (corrected outside air temperature), i.e. **SAT**. Sensors: through-the-window types in light aircraft, flush bulbs, or shielded probes.
- **Thermocouple gauges**: hot junction at the probe, cold junction at the instrument (millivoltmeter); **self-generating**.
- **EGT**: on the hottest cylinder's exhaust; used for **leaning**; essential for FADEC fuel-injected engines.
- **CHT**: thermocouple (iron or copper–constantan) in the hottest cylinder head, up to **400 °C**.
- **Oil and carburettor temperature**: not thermocouples — a **vapour-pressure Bourdon tube** (methyl chloride) or a **resistance (ratio-meter)** sensor, which is insensitive to voltage changes.
- **Colours**: green normal, amber caution, red limits. **Carburettor air temperature** gauge (−50 to +150 °C): **yellow −10 to +15 °C = icing**, **green +15 to +40 °C = normal**, **red above +40 °C = detonation**.

### RPM (tachometers)
- Piston engines: **crankshaft** speed; gas turbines: **compressor** speed.
- **Mechanical (magnetic) tacho**: flexible drive turning a magnet inside an aluminium **drag cup**; eddy currents drag the cup against a hairspring — small piston engines.
- **Electrical tacho-generator**: engine-driven generator; **DC** types lose voltage over long wires; **AC** types read **frequency** and are immune to wire length — more reliable. On larger piston engines an alternator drives a **synchronous motor** and drag cup.
- **Inductive (tacho) probe**: pulses from a phonic wheel — **turbofans**; modern pick-ups feed a digital counter.
- **Colours**: green normal, amber caution, **red line maximum RPM**; red arcs for ranges restricted by **vibration**.

### Fuel flow
- **Independent system**: a **spring-loaded vane** in the feed line → potentiometer or a.c. synchro → milliammeter calibrated in flow; a **bypass valve** opens if the vane sticks (may show a warning).
- **Integrated system**: a **turbine impeller** whose speed ∝ flow, with an **integrator** giving **flow (kg/h) and fuel used (kg)**; on turbines it sits in the **HP line between the FCU and the burners**; may give a low-flow warning.

### Fuel quantity
- **Mass** (kg/lb) is more useful than volume: it gives weight and energy content.
- **Float type**: float on a variable resistance; accurate only in **steady straight and level flight**; errors from fuel movement, attitude, acceleration, temperature (expansion) and **voltage** fluctuations.
- **Capacitance type**: concentric tubes; fuel replaces air as the **dielectric**; many probes averaged, accurate in turbulence, no high voltage in the tank. Fuel expansion is offset by its lower permittivity, so it reads **mass**. **Water** in the tank (higher density and dielectric constant) upsets the compensator and makes it **over-read**.

### Torque
- Power = **torque × RPM**. Essential on **turboprops** to protect the engine and reduction gear. A windmilling propeller gives **negative torque**.
- **Hydro-mechanical** torquemeter: pistons moved in proportion to the turning moment; the oil pressure produced is shown as torque. **Electronic**: **strain gauges** in the drive train.
- Units: **N·m**, J/rad, lb·ft. Colours: red limits, green normal, sometimes a **yellow arc at the low end** warning of negative torque.

### Exam traps
- **Bourdon tube = pressure** (higher pressures); capsule/bellows for low pressure.
- Temperature methods: **thermocouple, resistance, liquid (mercury)** — not reactance.
- **Thermocouple needs no power**; oil temperature uses **resistance or vapour pressure**.
- CAT gauge: **yellow −10 to +15 °C icing**, red above +40 °C detonation.
- Capacitance gauge → **mass**; water makes it over-read.

## 25. Basics of Electronic Displays (FMS, EFIS, EICAS/ECAM)
*Part II — Aircraft Instruments, Chapter 9.*

### Flight management system (FMS)
- "Pilot-interactive navigational computing and display systems" that fly a pre-planned lateral and vertical route with **maximum economy and safety**, reducing crew workload, especially in terminal areas.
- Components: **FMC** (flight management and guidance computer — 3-D position, performance); **MCDU/CDU** (the pilot interface); **flight control unit** (lateral and vertical commands); **source selector**; **display system**. Basically two units: **FMC and CDU**. Large airliners carry **twin** systems.
- The FMC stores the **navigation database**, updated every **28 days**: airports (ICAO 4-letter codes, position, runways, approach aid frequencies and idents, DHs, SIDs, STARs), waypoints (position, variation, holds), navaids (type, position, frequency, elevation), airways; plus a **performance database** (lift, drag, fuel flows, thrusts).
- CDU keys: **function keys** (select the display), **alphanumeric keys** (data entry), **line keys** (interaction with the display). FMS use divides into **route management** and **performance management**.

### EFIS
- The "glass cockpit": colour screens replace mechanical instruments. Two screens per pilot (~5 inches square):
  - **EADI / PFD**: attitude, airspeed, altitude, azimuth — the **main flying instrument**: flight director bars, speed tape (**left side**), ILS localiser and glideslope, autothrottle and AFDS modes (FMA), radio altitude, DH, groundspeed, slip indicator. **Altimeter setting** is shown on the PFD.
  - **EHSI / ND**: navigation information. PFD/ND are Airbus terms; **EADI/EHSI** Boeing.
- An **MFD** may add weather radar; others show systems and engines (ECAM, EICAS), crew alerting, electronic library. Airbus EFIS = **PFD + ND**.
- **Colours**: **white** — scales, labels, present/pilot-selected data; **cyan** — IFR database items, **inactive** route (long dashes); **magenta** — computed/derived data, **active waypoint and route**, FD commands, deviation pointers; **green** — normal/engaged, valid operation, ILS frequency; **yellow/amber** — **cautions** (alerts, failures, CDI at full scale, DH flashing); **red** — **warnings**, limits needing immediate action; **brown** — ground; **blue** — sky/water; **grey/black** — backgrounds.
- **Decision height**: set by the pilot on the **ADI section of the EFIS control panel**; shown on the EADI; **flashes yellow on reaching DH**. **Radio altitude** is digital, then below **1000 ft AGL** becomes a **circular scale** erased anticlockwise as the aircraft descends.
- **Heading reference**: **magnetic between 73°N and 65°S**, true above these latitudes; the EHSI can show **true or magnetic**.

### EHSI / ND modes
- **MAP**: aircraft at the bottom, **track up**; can show weather (WXR), waypoints, navaids, airports, route data, tuned VOR/ADF bearings, holds; **distance to go top left**, ETA, wind (bottom left), trend vector (30/60/90 s), altitude range arc, T/C, T/D, vertical deviation (±400 ft).
- **CENTRE MAP**: aircraft in the middle — shows what is behind.
- **NAV / expanded NAV**: expanded puts the aircraft at the bottom so weather can be shown.
- **PLAN**: the FMC flight plan, **true-north-up**, static; **no weather radar** and **no wind**.
- **VOR / ILS** (full or expanded): chosen by the tuned frequency; **weather radar only in expanded** modes (and MAP). Wind is shown in MAP, expanded and full VOR/ILS.
- **Route lines**: **active — solid magenta**; modified — white short dashes; inactive — **cyan long dashes**; offset — dash-dot.
- Weather returns: **green** light, **amber** more, **red/magenta** most intense. WXR is controlled from **both pilots' EFIS panels** and can show on both EHSIs at once.

### EICAS (Boeing) and ECAM (Airbus)
- **EICAS — Engine Indicating and Crew Alerting System**: sensors → computers (one is enough) → **upper display: primary engine data + warnings and cautions**; **lower display: secondary engine or system data**. One display fails → the other shows primary data in **compressed** form; both fail → **standby engine indicators**. Modes: **operational, status, maintenance** — maintenance on the **ground only**.
- **ECAM — Electronic Centralised Aircraft Monitoring**: data on the **aircraft systems**, in **checklist (left) and synoptic diagram (right)** form. Modes: **normal, advisory** (automatic, non-emergency change), **failure** (automatic, overrides all), **manual** — all available in flight.

### Exam traps
- PFD = **piloting**; ND = navigation. Boeing **EADI + EHSI**, Airbus **PFD + ND**.
- **Magenta = active**, cyan = inactive, amber = caution, red = warning.
- DH set on the **EFIS control panel (ADI section)**, flashes **yellow at DH**; RA circular below **1000 ft**.
- Magnetic north between **73°N and 65°S**.
- Weather radar **not in PLAN** (only MAP and expanded VOR/ILS). Distance to go **top left**.
- EICAS: **upper primary, lower secondary**; maintenance mode ground only. ECAM: checklist left, diagram right.
- Nav database **28 days**.


## 26. Basic Radio Theory
*Part III — Radio Navigation, Chapter 1.*

### Electromagnetic waves
- A changing electric field produces a perpendicular magnetic field, and the other way round; together they travel through space at the speed of light as **electromagnetic (EM) waves**. Around a radiating aerial there is **an electrical and a magnetic field**.
- **Frequency**: the number of waves passing a point per second (1 cycle per second = 1 Hz; kHz = 10³, MHz = 10⁶, GHz = 10⁹ Hz).
- **Wavelength (λ)**: the distance a wave covers while travelling through 360°.
- **λ = c ÷ f**, c = **3 × 10⁸ m/s**. Higher frequency → shorter wavelength, and vice versa.
- **Phase difference**: the angular difference between two waves of the same frequency.
- **Polarisation**: the direction in which the **electrical** component lies (the magnetic component is perpendicular to it).
- **Polar diagram**: the aerial characteristics of a transmitter or receiver; for a transmitter it is drawn to where signal strength has fallen to **50%**.

### Transmission and modulation
- **Continuous wave (CW)**: continuous transmission, **separate aerials** for transmitter and receiver, working independently.
- **Pulse modulation**: short pulses (primary and secondary radar); **one aerial** shared, switching between transmit and receive.
- **Modulation**: superimposing audio frequency or information on a carrier wave. Three types:
- **Amplitude modulation (AM)**: carrier amplitude varies with the amplitude of the audio, frequency constant. VHF communications are AM. Transmitter complicated, receiver simple; **susceptible to static and noise** (static = external disturbance, noise = within the system).
- **Frequency modulation (FM)**: carrier frequency varies with the audio frequency, amplitude constant. Transmitter simpler, receiver complicated; **free from static and noise**; drawback: **less range for the same power**.
- **Phase modulation**: carrier phase changed at fixed intervals (**used in GPS**).

### Frequency bands
| Band | Frequency | Wavelength | Name |
|---|---|---|---|
| VLF | 3–30 kHz | 100–10 km | Myriametric |
| LF | 30–300 kHz | 10–1 km | Kilometric |
| MF | 300 kHz–3 MHz | 1 km–100 m | Hectometric |
| HF | 3–30 MHz | 100–10 m | Decametric |
| VHF | 30–300 MHz | 10–1 m | Metric |
| UHF | 300 MHz–3 GHz | 1 m–10 cm | Decimetric |
| SHF | 3–30 GHz | 10–1 cm | Centimetric |
| EHF | 30–300 GHz | 1 cm–1 mm | Millimetric |
- Radar bands: **L 1–2 GHz, S 2–4 GHz, C 4–8 GHz, X 8–12 GHz**.

### Properties and propagation
- EM waves are **refracted, reflected, diffracted and attenuated** (attenuation = loss of amplitude). Passing between media both **direction and speed** change; they **bend towards the denser medium** (towards the normal going from less dense to denser).
- **Ground waves** (everything not refracted by the ionosphere):
- **Direct (line-of-sight) wave**: VHF, UHF, SHF, EHF. **LOS range (NM) = 1.25 (√H_tx + √H_rx)**, heights in feet.
- **Surface wave**: follows the earth's curvature by **diffraction**; intensity falls quickly; only for low frequencies (up to about **2 MHz**): VLF, LF, MF. Attenuation **rises with frequency** and is **greater over land than sea**.
- **Ground-reflected wave**: arrives after reflection from the ground.
- **Duct propagation**: VHF/UHF/SHF trapped under a **temperature inversion with humidity decreasing rapidly with height**; travels hundreds of miles (interference from distant VHF stations in high-pressure weather).
- Drawbacks of low frequency: **static**, **large aerials**, **high transmitter power**.
- **Sky wave (ionospheric)**: refracted back from the ionosphere; **2–30 MHz** (MF and HF). Below about 10 MHz works best **at night**; above 10 MHz **by day**.

### Ionosphere
- Layers of charged particles made by **UV radiation**. **D layer 50–100 km (avg 75)**, **E layer 100–150 km (avg 125)**, **F layer** above (splits into F1/F2 on summer days).
- Electron density is **greatest in the F layer, least in D**. At night the **D layer disappears** and E-layer density falls, so the ionosphere is effectively **higher at night**.
- **Skip distance**: transmitter → first returning sky wave. **Dead space**: end of the surface wave → first returning sky wave (no signal at all).
- **Critical angle**: the angle of incidence at which the first sky wave returns (the **critical ray**); at smaller angles the wave escapes.
- **Fading**: variation of signal strength at the receiver; increases with sky-wave frequency.
- **HF at night**: the wave must penetrate further before it bends back → skip distance grows and the receiver falls into dead space → **halve the HF frequency at night**. "**Sun up, frequency up; sun down, frequency down.**"
- Raising frequency (same conditions): **less refraction → longer skip distance → bigger dead space**.
- VLF/LF are absorbed by the layers; **VHF and above pass through**; part of MF and HF is reflected back.

### Operating frequencies

- **Cone of confusion (null zone)**: a cone over NDB, VDF and VOR stations with no usable signal; needles behave erratically.

### Exam traps
- λ = c/f: **12 mm → 25 GHz**, **3.5 cm → 8.57 GHz**, **19 cm → 1.579 GHz (UHF)**, **121.95 MHz → 2.46 m**, **373 kHz → about 800 m**.
- VOR, VHF and ILS localiser are **metric**; weather radar is **centimetric**; NDB is **kilometric/hectometric**.
- **295 kHz is LF** (LF ends at 300 kHz), not MF.
- Skip distance is **greater by night**; HF frequency must be **decreased at night**.
- Dead space is **between** the end of the surface wave and the first sky wave; skip distance is measured **from the transmitter**.
- Ducting: **inversion + humidity decreasing with height**.
- The book's grid gives "refraction increases with frequency"; physically refraction **decreases** as frequency rises (that is why skip distance grows) — Glidepath's bank follows the physics and notes the book key.

## 27. ADF / NDB
*Part III — Radio Navigation, Chapter 2 (with Chapter 3, Track and Drift Questions).*

### The equipment
- **ADF** (Automatic Direction Finder) = aircraft receiver; **NDB** (Non-Directional Beacon) = ground transmitter. The ADF receives NDBs and broadcast stations in about **190–1750 kHz**; NDBs use **190–450 kHz** (LF/MF). **500 kHz** (maritime distress) is not used for NDBs; 530–1750 kHz AM broadcast stations can also be tuned.
- The ADF is a separate set from the NAV/COM radio. An NDB can carry voice to the aircraft, but the pilot **cannot reply** on the ADF.
- Uses: **homing, holding, approach and en-route** navigation.
- **Principle**: relative bearing by **loop theory / switched cardioids**.
- Control panel: **OFF**; **ANT (REC)** — not automatic, used for **tuning**; **ADF (COMP)** — automatic direction finding; **BFO (CW)** — makes the ident audible; frequency selector; **TEST** button.
- **RBI** (relative bearing indicator) shows the bearing of the station from the nose.

### Emission types
- **NON A1A**: continuous unmodulated carrier (NON) for direction finding, periodically replaced by **interrupted carrier (A1A)** keyed with the Morse ident. Used for **long-range, high-power** NDBs. Needs the **BFO** to hear the ident.
- **NON A2A**: ident carried by **keyed tone amplitude modulation (A2A)**; medium-range NDBs. **BFO on for tuning, off for identification.**
- **BFO (beat frequency oscillator)**: generates a signal slightly removed from the received one (e.g. 262 kHz against 260 kHz); mixing gives 260, 262, 522 and **2 kHz** — the 2 kHz beat is the audible tone.

### How it works
- Two aerials: a **loop** (directional) and a **sense** aerial.
- Loop aligned with the NDB: the vertical arms receive the signal in different phase → maximum EMF; loop at 90° to the NDB → **zero EMF (null)**.
- A loop alone has **180° ambiguity** (direction property error), resolved by the **sense aerial**.
- Polar diagrams: loop = **figure of 8**; sense = **circle**; combined = **cardioid** (one maximum, one minimum). The **null** of the cardioid drives the RBI pointer; the loop is kept with its null 90° to the station.
- Bearings: **true** (from true north), **magnetic** (from magnetic north), **relative** (from the nose/heading).

### Range
- **Range ∝ √power**: to double the range, **quadruple the power**.
- Sea gives more range than land; **lower frequency gives more range**.
- Night effect limits NDB range to about **70 NM**; the **DOC** (designated operational coverage) also limits it.
- Accuracy (day only, within DOC): **±2° up to 70 NM, ±5° beyond**.
- **Locator NDB**: **15 W, 10–25 NM, NON A2A**, co-located with ILS markers. **Homing/holding/aerodrome NDB**: **200 W, 50 NM, NON A2A**. **Long-range NDB**: no fixed range.

### Errors
- **Night effect**: at night sky waves appear in the MF band and hit the loop's horizontal arms → the null is distorted, the needle wanders, signal fades. **Maximum at dawn and dusk.** Reduce it: use a **lower-frequency** beacon, one **within 70 NM**, a **high-power** beacon.
- **Coastal refraction**: the wave speeds up over the (less dense) sea and **bends away from the normal, towards the land**. Worst when the bearing crosses the coast at an **acute angle** and the beacon is **far inland**; least when it crosses at 90°. Reduce it: fly **high**, use beacons whose signal crosses at **90°**, beacons **near the coast**, **higher power**.
- **Quadrantal error**: the aircraft's metal and fields bend the signal before the loop; **maximum on quadrantal (inter-cardinal) relative bearings** (045, 135, 225, 315). Corrected with a **quadrantal correction card**: **RB = LSR ± QC** (LSR = loop scale reading).
- **Interference**: from other NDBs; prevented by the **DOC** published in the AIP, valid **by day only** (protected range).
- **Mountain effect**: hills reflect and re-radiate LF/MF; low-flying aircraft get a composite, erroneous bearing.
- **Static / precipitation**: thunderstorms (CB) and precipitation reduce range and accuracy — the **greatest inaccuracy** in ADF bearings; the needle may point at a CB.
- **No failure warning**: most ADFs have **no failure flag**, so failures can go unnoticed.
- **Bank angle (dip)**: bank induces EMF in the loop's horizontal elements.

### Track and drift (Chapter 3)
- **QDM** (MBT): magnetic track to the station; **QDR** (MBF): magnetic bearing from the station (= radial); **QUJ** (TBT): true track to; **QTE** (TBF): true bearing from.
- **QDM = QDR ± 180°**; **QUJ = QDM ± variation**; **QTE = QDR ± variation = QUJ ± 180°**.
- **MBT = MH + RB**; **RB = MBT − MH** (add/subtract 360 as needed).
- **TMG = H + drift**; **TMG = RT + TE** (starboard +, port −). TMG right of heading = starboard drift.
- Winds are **true**; aerodrome METAR/ATIS winds and runway headings are **magnetic**; tracks are true unless stated; ATS routes magnetic.
- Homing with RB 000 in a crosswind gives a **curved path**; heading **decreasing** means the wind pushes you right (**right / starboard drift**).
- **Bearing accuracy classes**: A ±2°, B ±5°, C ±10°, D more than 10°. **Position classes**: A 5 NM, B 20 NM, C 50 NM, D more than 50 NM.
- Off-track sums: **TE (°) = off-track distance × 60 ÷ distance**; RB to an NDB ahead = drift ± TE.

### Exam traps
- Long-range NDBs use **NON A1A**; BFO needed to hear an **A1A** ident.
- Quadrantal error peaks at **045/135/225/315 relative** — e.g. heading 315°, a signal from 360° (M) arrives at 045° relative.
- Coastal refraction: an NDB **50 NM inland** gives a bigger error over the sea than one 20 NM inland; **acute angle** is worst.
- To double NDB range: **×4 power**.
- Outbound with RB 184 (4° starboard drift) → inbound on the reciprocal hold **RB 356**.
- The promulgated range (DOC) applies **by day only**.
- ADF has **no** failure warning; VOR and ILS have flags.

## 28. VOR
*Part III — Radio Navigation, Chapter 4 (with Chapter 5, VOR/RMI/ADF Related Questions).*

### Basics
- **VHF Omni-directional Range**: line-of-sight en-route aid, **108.00–117.95 MHz**, **160 channels**. 108–112 MHz shared with the ILS localiser: **odd first decimal = localiser, even = VOR** ("**LOVE**"); **112–117.95 MHz VOR only**.
- Usually part of a **NAV/COM** set. The pilot cannot transmit on a NAV radio, but ATS can transmit on a VOR frequency (ATIS, or to reach an aircraft with radio failure).
- Uses: magnetic bearings by day and night; airways, routes, approaches, holding, radial interceptions, track keeping and position.
- **Principle: phase comparison of two 30 Hz signals.** **Radial**: a magnetic track radiating out from the station.

### Conventional VOR (CVOR)
- **Reference signal**: 30 Hz **frequency-modulated**, omni-directional (polar diagram a **circle**), same phase in all directions.
- **Variable signal**: 30 Hz **amplitude-modulated** by an aerial rotating **clockwise at 30 rev/s (1800 rpm)**; polar diagram a **rotating figure of 8**.
- Both are **in phase on magnetic north**; the phase difference at any point = the **magnetic bearing from the station (QDR)** (e.g. QTE 220°, variation 10° W → QDR 230° → phase difference 230°). They are 90° out of phase on magnetic east.
- Combined polar diagram: a **limaçon** (made at the ground; no sharp null), unlike the ADF **cardioid** (made at the aircraft; well-defined null).
- A radial is a **great circle** path.

### Doppler VOR (DVOR)
- For sites with uneven terrain: about **50 aerials**, reasonably **free of site error**. Reference signal **AM**, variable signal **FM**, rotation **anticlockwise**. Same frequencies, same airborne receiver, same phase-comparison principle.

### Types
- **Standard VOR**: airway centre lines, about **200 W**. **Terminal VOR (TVOR)**: low power, aerodrome location / procedural approach with DME. **Broadcast VOR (BVOR)**: voice (ATIS) superimposed.
- **VOT** (VOR test facility): low-power, transmits **only the 360° radial** (phase difference 180° in all directions). Centre the CDI: **000° ± 4° FROM** or **180° ± 4° TO** ("Cessna 182"). A VOT can be used **anywhere on the ground**; a VOR receiver check is done **at designated points**.

### Accuracy and monitoring
- Ground signal **±3° (95%)**; VOT check **±4°**; airborne check **±6°**; overall **±5° (95%)**.
- Ident every **10 s**. The ident is **removed** (or the station switched off) if the bearing is wrong by more than **1°**, the monitor fails, or power drops **15%** below normal.
- **Cone of confusion**: no signal above the station (radiation at about 40–80° elevation): TO/FROM flickers, CDI swings, warning flag may show.
- **Warning flag** shows with: no/low aircraft power, aircraft equipment failure, ground station failure, indicator failure, weak reference or variable signal, during tuning.

### Errors ("SAP" aggregate + two more)
- **Site error**: buildings, uneven terrain, trees, wires, fences, long grass (course displacement); max **1°** or the ident is removed.
- **Airborne equipment error**: receiver inaccuracy; max **±1°**.
- **Propagation error**: reflection, refraction, diffraction (uneven ground). Site + airborne + propagation = **aggregate error**.
- **Beacon alignment error**: reference/variable signals not aligned with magnetic north.
- **Pilotage error**: radials bunch up close to the station; the CDI is very sensitive.
- No night effect, no quadrantal error.

### Range
- **Range ∝ √power**; **LOS = 1.25 (√H_tx + √H_rx)**, or **12.5 √FL** for a station at MSL.
- **DOC** given in **range and altitude** (ADF: range only); within it, freedom from interference by co-channel VORs is guaranteed. Beyond it: **interference from other transmitters**.

### The indicator (OBS, CDI, TO/FROM)
- **OBS**: selects any of the 360 magnetic tracks. **CDI**: deviation from the selected track; **2° per dot**, full scale **10°** (5 dots).
- **TO/FROM** does **not** show whether you are heading to or from the station; it depends only on the **OBS selection** and the **aircraft's position**: would flying the selected track take you to or away from the station?
- **Alarm flag** with receiver/power failure, no usable signal (range, height, overhead or **abeam** the station), ground station failure.
- **Reverse sensing**: when the selected track is roughly opposite to the heading, the needle shows the wrong way. To go **inbound on a radial**, set the **reciprocal** of the radial with **TO** (e.g. inbound on the 104 radial: **284 TO**).
- Variation for VOR bearings: always at the **VOR**.

### RMI (Chapter 5)
- Compass card slaved to the **gyro-magnetic compass** (shows **magnetic heading**); needles show **QDM** (head) and **QDR** (tail).
- **ADF needle**: QDM made by adding magnetic heading and relative bearing mechanically; **VOR needle**: fed straight from the VOR phase measurement (so it stays correct even if the card fails).
- Gives: magnetic heading, relative bearing, QDM/QDR, radial, fix.
- To make true bearings: **NDB → variation at the aircraft; VOR → variation at the beacon**.
- RMI card stuck at 090°: the VOR tail still gives the **radial**; the relative bearing is unknown. ADF pointer 200° with card stuck at 090° → RB = 110°.

### Exam traps
- Phase difference = **QDR (magnetic bearing FROM)**. 30° difference → radial **030**.
- Radial from a position: use the **VOR's** variation (aircraft due south of a VOR with 13° E → radial 167).
- Inbound on a radial: OBS = **radial ± 180 with TO**.
- 1° at 200 NM ≈ **3.5 NM**; 5 NM at 100 NM = 3° = **1.5 dots**.
- A VOR radial is a **great circle**, not a rhumb line.
- DVOR's advantage: **reduced site error**.
- VOT: **360 FROM / 180 TO**, tolerance ±4°.

## 29. Holding, Radial Intercept, Bearing to Plot
*Part III — Radio Navigation, Chapter 6.*

### Standard holding pattern
- **Right turns, rate 1 (3°/s)**, **1-minute** legs up to **FL140**, **1½ minutes** above FL140 (about 4 and 6 minutes a circuit).
- Terms (for an inbound track of 270°): inbound leg/track 270°; holding (inbound) radial 090°; outbound leg/heading 090°.
- **Holding speeds (IAS)**: up to FL140 **230 kt**; FL140–200 **240 kt**; FL200–340 **265 kt**; above FL340 **M0.83**.
- **Non-standard hold**: the mirror image, **left turns**; timing may differ.

### Entry sectors (±5° flexibility on the boundaries)
- **Sector 1 — parallel**: fly outbound on the **published inbound track** (on the non-holding side) for 1 minute, then turn **left** (in a right-hand hold) back to the fix/inbound track.
- **Sector 2 — offset (teardrop)**: fly a track **30° off the reciprocal** of the inbound track, on the holding side, for 1 minute, then turn back onto the inbound track.
- **Sector 3 — direct**: turn straight into the pattern on crossing the fix.
- Sizes: **direct 180°, teardrop 70°, parallel 110°**. For a right-hand hold, measure the aircraft's track to the fix against the inbound track: from −70° to +110° direct; +110° to +180° teardrop; +180° to +290° parallel. Book method: draw the sectors on the holding radial (holding radial − 70°, + 110°).
- At VOR intersections or VOR/DME fixes, entries are limited to the radials or DME arcs that form the fix.

### Wind corrections in the hold
- **Drift**: inbound leg — apply the **actual drift**; outbound leg — apply **three times the drift**. Rate of turn is unchanged.
- **Time**: outbound leg only — **±1 second per knot** of headwind/tailwind component.

### Radial intercepts
- Standard intercept angles **30°, 60°, 90°**. If not given: **intercept angle = 2 × (difference between present and final MBT/MBF)**, rounded **up** to 30/60/90.
- **Heading to intercept = final MBT/MBF ± intercept angle** (pick the practical one, towards the new track).
- **RB at the intercept = final MBT − heading** (add 360 if negative). This is the relative bearing that "confirms interception".
- Turning off by an angle and rotating the OBS by the same angle makes an isosceles triangle: time to the station = time taken for the bearing to change.

### Bearing to plot (BTP)
- Bearings are always **plotted from the station**, and only **true bearings (QTE)** are plotted.
- VOR: centre the CDI with **FROM** showing; the OBS reading is the QDR. RMI gives QDR for both VOR and ADF.
- **Variation**: ADF — use variation **at the aircraft**; VOR and other ground beacons — **at the station**; mean heading — mean variation.
- **QTE = QDM ± var ± 180°** or **LSR + QC + true heading ± 180°**.
- **Lambert (and polar stereographic) chart**: no correction, **BTP = QTE**.
- **Mercator chart**: apply the **conversion angle (CA = ½ d.long × sin mean lat)**: **BTP = QTE ± CA**, where the change of longitude makes it necessary.

### Exam traps
- Outbound leg: **3 × drift**, **1 s per knot**; inbound leg: actual drift.
- Holding speed **230 kt** up to FL140; legs **1 min** up to FL140, **1½ min** above.
- Intercept angle: **double the difference, round up** (20° → 60°, 40° → 90°).
- Plot from the **station**, in **true**. Lambert: no conversion angle.
- ADF bearings: variation at the **aircraft**; VOR: at the **station**.

## 30. ILS
*Part III — Radio Navigation, Chapter 7.*

### Components
- **Guidance**: localiser (lateral, VHF) and glide path (vertical, UHF). Tuning the localiser frequency **automatically tunes the paired glide path** (one selection, one ident).
- **Range**: outer and middle marker beacons, or a low-power **DME co-located with the glide path**.
- **Visual**: approach, touchdown-zone, centre-line and runway lights.

### Localiser
- **108–112 MHz, odd first decimals** (108.1, 109.15, 110.3…), VHF, shares the VOR receiver.
- **Principle: lobe comparison / difference in depth of modulation (DDM)**: two overlapping lobes on the **same frequency** with different modulation: **90 Hz (yellow) on the left**, **150 Hz (blue) on the right** of the centre line as seen on the approach.
- Coverage: **±10° to 25 NM**, **±35° to 17 NM**; vertically up to **7°** (from the localiser). Outside the coverage you may get **false course** indications.
- Aerial at the **far (non-approach) end**, about **300 m beyond the runway on the extended centre line**. Some localisers radiate a **back beam** behind the aerial.
- CDI: turn towards the needle; **0.5° per dot** on a five-dot display (full scale 2.5°).

### Glide path
- **329.3–335 MHz, UHF**, DDM; nominally **3°**. **90 Hz below** the path, **150 Hz above**.
- Coverage: **8° each side** of the centre line to at least **10 NM**; vertically **0.45θ to 1.75θ** (1.35°–5.25° for 3°; down to 0.30θ where needed).
- Correct with **power** (rate of descent), not by pointing the nose.
- **False glide paths** come from the multiple lobes in the vertical plane (ground reflection); they lie **above** the true path, the first at about **6°** for a 3° path. Signs: high rate of descent, height over the OM higher than published, flag may show.
- **ILS reference point**: the point at a specified height (about **50 ft**) above the threshold through which the glide path passes.

### Marker beacons (75 MHz)
| Marker | Distance | Audio | Light |
|---|---|---|---|
| Outer (OM) | 3.5–6 NM | **400 Hz dashes**, 2 per second | **Blue** |
| Middle (MM) | about 3500 ft | **1300 Hz dot-dash**, 3 per second | **Amber** |
| Inner (IM) | about 1000 ft | **3000 Hz dots**, 6 per second | **White** |
- The OM marks the glide-path intercept at the published height; the MM is near the Cat I decision height (about 200 ft); the IM (about 1000 ft out) marks the **Cat II** decision point (book: about 50 ft). The **inner marker is optional**.
- Ident: Morse, usually starting with **I**, **1020 Hz** tone, from the **localiser**; removed on failure (may be kept for localiser-only approaches with a warning).
- **ILS/DME** ranges are zero-referenced to the **threshold**; use only within the localiser coverage, up to about **6000 ft**.

### Categories and minima
| Cat | DH | RVR |
|---|---|---|
| I | ≥ 60 m (200 ft) | 800 m vis / 550 m RVR |
| II | 30 to < 60 m | 300 m |
| IIIA | 0 to < 30 m | 175 m |
| IIIB | 0 to < 15 m | 50 m |
| IIIC | 0 | 0 |
- Cat I guidance to **200 ft**, Cat II to **100 ft**, Cat III to the **runway surface**.
- With the glide path out: non-precision (localiser only, MDA).
- **RVR**: the distance a pilot on the centre line can see runway centre-line markings or lights.
- **Holding positions** for Cat II/III are further from the runway to keep aircraft out of the **ILS sensitive area** (aircraft would disturb the signals).

### Rate of descent sums
- On 3°: **ROD ≈ 5 × groundspeed** (ft/min); exact **GS (kt) × 101.3 × tan θ**. Height on 3° ≈ **300–318 ft per NM** (+ 50 ft threshold crossing).
- Every 10 kt change in GS ≈ **50 ft/min** change in ROD. Headwind up → GS down → ROD down.
- Distance to intercept: height ÷ 300 (≈ 2500 ft → 8.3 NM; ÷ 318 → 7.8 NM).

### Exam traps
- **90 Hz: left and below → fly right and up.** 150 Hz: right and above.
- Localiser **odd** decimals 108–112; glide path **UHF** 329–335 MHz; markers **75 MHz**.
- OM **blue, 400 Hz dashes**; MM **amber, 1300 Hz dot-dash**; IM **white, 3000 Hz dots**.
- Glide path azimuth: **8° each side to 10 NM**; localiser **±10° to 25 NM, ±35° to 17 NM**.
- False glide paths are **above** the true one (first at ~6°).
- The book's key for "more 90 Hz than 150 Hz" prints *right and down*; by its own lobe rules the answer is **right and up**.

## 31. Radar, SSR and DME
*Part III — Radio Navigation, Chapter 8.*

### Primary radar
- Radio Detection And Ranging. **Primary**: echo principle (range) + searchlight principle (bearing = where the aerial points). Pulse radar uses **one aerial** for transmit and receive; **CW radar** has **no minimum range**.
- **Pulse width (length)**: start to end of one pulse. **PRP/PRI**: start of one pulse to the start of the next. **PRF = 1/PRI** (pulses per second).
- **Range = c × t ÷ 2** (out and back). 1 µs of echo time ≈ 150 m of range.
- **Maximum range = c ÷ (2 × PRF)**: the **higher the PRF, the shorter** the maximum range (each pulse must return before the next).
- **Minimum range = c × PW ÷ 2** (e.g. 3 µs → 450 m). **Range resolution** also = c × PW ÷ 2 (2 µs → 300 m ≈ 984 ft).
- **Azimuth resolution**: targets are smeared by one **beam width**; two targets need to be a beam width apart (1 in 60: 3° beam → 2 NM apart shows separately at **40 NM**). Narrower beam = better.
- **Power ∝ range⁴**: double the range → **16 ×** power.

### Radars by type
| Radar | Frequency | Wavelength / notes |
|---|---|---|
| En-route surveillance | ~600 MHz | 50 cm, up to 250 NM, 5–15 rpm, little rain clutter |
| Terminal area surveillance | 600–3000 MHz | 10–20 cm, about 75 NM |
| Aerodrome (approach) surveillance | — | about 25 NM |
| Precision approach radar | 9–10 GHz | 3 cm (military fields) |
| Surface movement (ASDE) | SHF 3–10+ GHz | ~3 cm, up to 60 rpm, 2.5–6 NM |
| SSR | 1030 / 1090 MHz | 27 cm |
| Airborne weather radar | 9.375 GHz (X band) | 3.2 cm |
| Radio altimeter | 4.2–4.4 GHz | 7 cm |
| Doppler | 8.75–8.85 / 13.25–13.4 GHz | — |
| DME | 960–1213 MHz | 27 cm |

### Airborne weather radar (AWR)
- **X band, about 9–10 GHz (3 cm)**: large drops and storms reflect well; fog, mist and stratus do not. Range up to about **150 NM**. Antenna gyro-stabilised (book: in pitch and yaw).
- Colours: **magenta/red heavy**, **yellow moderate**, **green light**.
- **Mapping**: **cosecant-squared beam** (more power to the far part of the beam).
- **Cloud height**: height difference = (tilt ± half beam width) ÷ 60 × range in ft (range NM × 6080). Top: lower edge where the echo just disappears.
- On the ground: operate only **with precautions for personnel** (and buildings).

### Doppler and MTI
- **Doppler effect**: received frequency rises when the target approaches, falls as it recedes. Once used for groundspeed and drift; now used in **MTI** radar to reject fixed or slow clutter.
- **Blind speeds** (MTI response zero): reduce by **lower frequency (longer wavelength)**, **high PRF**, staggered PRT, frequency diversity, jittered PRF.

### Secondary radar — SSR
- The target carries a **transponder** that replies, so transmitter power is lower (one-way trip) and replies can carry **coded information**.
- **Interrogator 1030 MHz, transponder 1090 MHz** (UHF); different frequencies avoid **self-triggering**.
- Interrogation pulse spacing: **Mode A 8 µs** (identity, **4096 codes**), **Mode B 17 µs** (not used), **Mode C 21 µs** (pressure altitude, **100 ft steps**, always referenced to **1013.25 hPa**), Mode D 25 µs (research).
- **Mode S**: everything Mode C does plus data link; altitude in **25 ft** steps; more than 4096 codes.
- Mode C/S not used if it differs by more than **±300 ft**.
- Switch: OFF – **SBY** (on, not replying: after start, before take-off, after landing) – ON (Mode A only) – **ALT** (Mode A + C) – TST.
- **IDENT** adds the **SPI pulse** for about **20 s**.
- Codes: **7500 hijack, 7600 radio failure, 7700 emergency**, 2000 no code allotted, 0000 transponder failure.
- **Garbling**: overlapping replies from aircraft closer than about **1.7 NM**; removed by a killer circuit.

### DME
- **Slant range** to the station; **UHF 962–1213 MHz**; secondary radar; **random (jittered) PRF** principle.
- Aircraft interrogates with **pulse pairs**; ground transponder replies after a fixed **50 µs delay** on a frequency **63 MHz** away (so it can't lock on to its own reflections).
- **Search mode 150 pps**; **lock-on (tracking) 24–30 pps, average 27**; if nothing is found, **standby / 60 pps**. A ground station handles up to **2700 pps**: **100 aircraft** locked on (**126** in search/standby); more than 100 → **saturation (swamping)**: the weakest/furthest lose lock.
- **Memory**: on losing the signal the range freezes for about **10 s**, then search resumes.
- **Range = (c × (T − 50 µs)) ÷ 2**.
- **Slant range error**: negligible when height is below **1000 ft per NM** of range. Overhead it shows the **height in NM** (FL360 ≈ 6 NM).
- **Ground range = √(slant² − height²)** (height in NM, ft ÷ 6080).
- **Groundspeed**: accurate only when tracking **directly to or from** the station (and far away at height).
- **Ident**: DME once every **30 s**, VOR every **10 s**: every fourth VOR ident replaced by the DME ident, at a **higher pitch**.
- **Accuracy**: **±0.25 NM + 1.25%** of distance (book table also: ±0.5 NM or 3%).
- **TACAN**: military azimuth + range; civil aircraft use only its **DME**.
- **ILS/DME**: zero at the threshold; reliable in the approach direction within the localiser coverage, up to about **6000 ft**.
- **Pairing with VOR**: co-located and serving the same station; may still be paired within **7 NM**; then the ident ends in **Z**. Same ident and "associated" when within **2000 ft (600 m)**.

### Exam traps
- Max range uses **PRF**, min range and range resolution use **pulse width**.
- 300 µs echo → **45 km** (book key prints 45 NM; its own working gives km).
- SSR **1030 up, 1090 down**; Mode C **1013.25 hPa**, 100 ft steps; Mode S 25 ft.
- DME saturation: **100** aircraft (126 in search); search **150 pps**, track **27 pps**.
- DME groundspeed is good only **directly to/from** the station; overhead it reads height.
- AWR: **3 cm X band**; doesn't see fog or stratus.
- MTI blind speeds: **lower frequency, higher PRF**.

## 32. GNSS
*Part III — Radio Navigation, Chapter 9.*

### Systems and orbits
- Satellite navigation systems: **GPS (NAVSTAR, USA)**, **GLONASS (Russia)**, Galileo, BeiDou, **NavIC (India)**.
- GNSS satellites use inclined **Medium Earth Orbits**; GPS at about **55°** to the equator.
- Three segments: **space**, **control**, **user**.

### GPS constellation
- **24 satellites**: **21 operational + 3 spares**, **6 orbital planes**, **4 per plane**, inclined **55°**, about **20 200 km** high (GPS orbits about **1200 km above GLONASS**, which is why they never collide).
- Orbital period **11 h 58 min** (twice a day). Each satellite carries **4 atomic clocks** (one working, three spares).
- At least **4–5 satellites** are always in view. Earth model: **WGS-84**.

### How a position is found
- **Principle**: simultaneous **ranges** from a number of satellites (range = signal travel time × c).
- Each satellite sends a unique **pseudo-random noise (PRN) code** of **1 ms**; the data give **satellite position (ephemeris)**, clock time, clock error, ionospheric information and other data.
- Ranges measured with the receiver's (less accurate) crystal clock are **pseudo-ranges**; **1 ms clock error ≈ 162 NM**.
- **1 satellite**: position in space (a sphere); **2**: ambiguous (circle); **3**: ambiguous (two points); **4**: a **3-D fix** (and the clock error solved); **5**: **RAIM**.
- **L1 1575.42 MHz**: **C/A (coarse acquisition) code** repeated every millisecond, plus P code; **L2 1227.6 MHz**: **P code** (encrypted = **Y code**). The precision code is modulated at 10.23 MHz and repeats every 7 days.

### RAIM
- **Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring**: checks the integrity of the GPS data (timely warning when it must not be used). Needs **5 satellites** (4 + 1), or **4 + baro-aiding** (altimeter setting entered). To **isolate and exclude** a faulty satellite: **6**, or 5 + baro.

### Errors
- **Ephemeris** (satellite position) ~2.5 m; **satellite clock** ~1.5 m (checked every 12 h).
- **Ionospheric delay**: the **largest** error; reduced with **two frequencies**; ~5 m single-frequency.
- **Tropospheric delay** ~0.3 m; **multipath** (careful aerial siting).
- **GDOP**: geometric dilution of precision — worst when the satellites are **close together** (poor cuts).
- **Vertical position**: height above the **WGS-84 ellipsoid** can differ from MSL altitude by hundreds of feet; vertical accuracy is worse than horizontal: use the altimeter.

### Augmentation and services
- **DGPS**: a reference station at a surveyed point compares its GPS position with the truth and broadcasts the **correction** to nearby receivers (1–3 m).
- **WAAS**: en route + Cat I-type approaches; **LAAS**: local, Cat II/III.
- **GAGAN** (GPS Aided GEO Augmented Navigation, India): reference stations (**INRES**) → master control (**INMCC**) → uplink (**INLUS**) → **geostationary satellites** broadcasting corrections on the **GPS frequency**.
- **NavIC (IRNSS)**: India's regional system, **7 satellites** in geosynchronous orbit, 3 atomic clocks each.
- **SPS**: C/A code, all users: **100 m horizontal (95%)**, 300 m (99.99%). **PPS**: C/A + P(Y) code, US DoD and authorised users: **16 m (98%)**.

### Pros and cons
- Advantages: worldwide, all speeds and altitudes, highly accurate, 24 h, continuous **3-D** position, unrestricted range, **entirely space-based**, with INS can replace ground aids, cheap portable sets.
- Disadvantages: a **military system** that could be interrupted; civil accuracy lower by policy; without RAIM no immediate warning of satellite malfunction.

### Exam traps
- Position by **ranges**, not bearings. **4** satellites for a 3-D fix, **5** for RAIM.
- **24** satellites (21 + 3), **6 planes × 4**, **55°**, ~20 200 km, period **~12 h**.
- Civil frequency **L1 1575.42 MHz**; earth model **WGS-84**.
- GDOP worst when satellites are **close together**.
- DGPS = the **correction** applied to the raw position.

### TCAS (from the summary table)
- Uses the **SSR principle** on 1030/1090 MHz, range about **30 NM**.
- **TCAS I: traffic advisories (TA) only**. **TCAS II: resolution advisories (RA) in the vertical plane only**.
- RAs: **corrective** (change vertical speed: climb/descend) or **preventive** (monitor vertical speed); RA generated about **40 s** before collision (book table). No RAs below about **1000 ft**.
- Symbols: hollow diamond — other traffic; solid white/blue diamond — proximate (within 6 NM, ±1200 ft); **yellow circle — TA**; **red square — RA**.
- Fly the RA on the VSI: keep the needle out of the red, "fly to the green".

### GPWS modes (from the summary table)
| Mode | Hazard | Envelope | Alert / warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Excessive rate of descent | 50–2450 ft AGL | "SINK RATE" → "WHOOP WHOOP PULL UP" |
| 2 | Excessive closure with rising terrain | below 1800 ft (not landing config), 790 ft (landing config) | "TERRAIN TERRAIN" → pull up |
| 3 | Height loss after take-off / go-around | 50–700 ft AGL | "DON'T SINK" |
| 4 | Unsafe landing configuration | gear up below 500 ft, flaps up below 200 ft | "TOO LOW GEAR / FLAPS" |
| 5 | Below glide slope | 100–200 ft and below | "GLIDE SLOPE" (no warning) |
| 6 | Decision height | selected height | "MINIMUMS" (no warning) |
| 7 (EGPWS) | Excessive bank / wind shear | bank on finals below 150 ft; wind shear below 1500 ft | "BANK ANGLE", "WIND SHEAR" |
- GPWS inputs: radio altimeter, VSI/air data, ILS glide slope, gear and flap positions.

### The book's formula sheet
- **True altitude** = PA + 4 × (actual − ISA temp) × PA/1000 (4% of PA per 10° deviation).
- **Density altitude** = PA + 118 × (actual − ISA temp).
- **TAS** ≈ RAS + 1.75% of RAS per 1000 ft.
- **TAT (K) = SAT (K) × (1 + 0.2 M²)**; ram rise = (TAS/100)².
- **Mach = TAS ÷ LSS**; **LSS = 39 √T (K)**.
- **QNH = QFE + elevation/30** (hPa, elevation in ft).
- **LOS range = 1.25 √H_tx + 1.25 √H_rx**, or **12.5 √FL**.
- **Distance to station** = TAS × minutes ÷ bearing change (°).
- **Departure = d.long × 60 × cos lat**; **convergency = d.long × sin mean lat**; **CA = ½ convergency**.
- **Gyro drift** = real + earth rate (**15 × sin lat**, as in the book's table; its formula sheet prints cos) + transport wander (GS/60 × tan lat).
- Fuel: litres × SG = kg; Imp gal × SG × 10 = lb; kg × 2.2 = lb; Imp gal × 1.2 = US gal; 100 L = 22 Imp gal = 26.4 US gal.

### Exam traps
- Know the **frequency and band** of every aid: ADF LF/MF, VOR/LOC/markers VHF, GP/DME/SSR/GPS UHF, rad-alt/MLS/AWR SHF.
- TCAS II resolves **only in the vertical**; TCAS I gives **TA only**.
- GPWS Mode 4: gear **500 ft**, flaps **200 ft**; Mode 5 glide slope and Mode 6 minimums are **alerts, not warnings**.
- Radio altimeter works to **2500 ft**.
